## What the document is

Card `8c22358135a85824` — **DOW-UAP-D092**, released by the Department of War as
"Committee to Review Project Bluebook, 1966–1967" — is not a single memo but an
85-page composite folder. <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={1} q="AD HOC COMMITTEE TO REVIEW" /> It
collects the working papers of the USAF Scientific Advisory Board ad hoc
committee, chaired by physicist Dr. Brian O'Brien, that the Air Force convened
in early 1966 to review Project Blue Book — the review whose recommendation
became the University of Colorado (Condon) study and, ultimately, the pretext
for closing Blue Book in 1969.

What makes the folder valuable is not the committee's tidy public recommendation
but the candor of the internal traffic around it. Read in sequence, the memos
show officials stating plainly what they wanted the panel *for*: not primarily to
find out what UFOs were, but to solve a public-relations problem the Air Force
felt it was losing.

## The request: an admission before a review

The chain begins with a 28 September 1965 memorandum from Major General E. B.
LeBailly, the Air Force Director of Information, to the Scientific Advisory
Board. Its factual core is a striking concession. As of 30 June 1965, the Air
Force had investigated 9,267 UFO reports —

> "Of these 9267 reports, 663 cannot be explained." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={22} q="Of these 9267 reports, 663 cannot be explained" />

And the unexplained residue could not simply be waved off as cranks. In the
Director of Information's own words:

> "...many of the reports that cannot be explained have come from intelligent and technically well qualified individuals whose integrity cannot be doubted." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={22} q="technically well qualified individuals whose integrity" />

On that basis LeBailly asked the Board to organize "a working scientific panel
composed of both physical and social scientists" <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={22} q="a working scientific panel" /> to
review Blue Book's "resources, methods, and findings." The signature is
LeBailly's. <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={23} q="E. B. LeBAILLY" /> The
inclusion of *social* scientists is the first hint that the problem, as the Air
Force understood it, was as much about people's reactions as about the objects.

## The candid brief: "get the Air Force off the hook"

The clearest statement of motive is a 21 January 1966 memo for the record —
"Some SAFOI Thoughts for the SAB Ad Hoc Committee on UFOs" — written by Major
Harold A. Steiner after he visited the Secretary of the Air Force Office of
Information (SAFOI). It frames the entire matter not as a scientific question but
as an image problem. SAFOI had answered more than 3,300 UFO letters the previous
year, and Steiner records that:

> "The problem essentially boils down to one that is typified by the question, 'When did you stop beating your wife?'" <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={42} q="When did you stop beating your wife?" />

Whatever the Air Force said, the memo explains, it lost: any answer "tends to
incriminate the Air Force as a concealer of information and at the very least
detracts from its image." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={42} q="detracts from its image" /> The
office needed a way out, and it was explicit about what kind of way out:

> "SAFOI wants some prestigious scientific group to look at the UFO problem in depth and come up with a position that will help get the Air Force off the hook." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={43} q="SAFOI wants some prestigious scientific group to look at" /> <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={43} q="help get the Air Force off the hook" />

The panel was to be prestigious precisely so that its conclusions would hold up
under public criticism — to let the Air Force "stand before the most reknown [*sic*]
critics" <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={43} q="stand before the most" /> and say its
position was sound. And the memo is careful about what it did *not* want: a bland
endorsement that would settle nothing.

> "...they do not want a 'for motherhood and against sin' type of committee report." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={43} q="motherhood and against sin" />

The memo closes by ranking the stakes: none of the Air Force's other
contemporary controversies — sonic boom, the supersonic transport, integration —
had "created the poor public image for the Air Force that the UFO problem does."
<Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={43} q="poor public image for the" /> This is
the document's thesis, stated by the Air Force about itself: the UFO file was, at
bottom, a perception problem.

## The panel at work: Sagan, statistics, and "99% public relations"

The committee met at Wright-Patterson on 3 February 1966. Its members were
serious scientists — O'Brien (chair), Launor Carter, Jesse Orlansky, Richard
Porter, Willis Ware, and a young **Dr. Carl Sagan** of the Smithsonian
Astrophysical Observatory. <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={7} q="Reviewed: Robertson Report, dated 17 January 1953" />

The verbatim meeting notes preserve individual proposals by name, and Sagan's are
methodologically the sharpest in the file. He pressed for rigor rather than
reassurance:

> "Along with the scientific approach the Air Force should conduct some unannounced controlled experiments to check out the techniques." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={38} q="conduct some unannounced controlled experiments to check" />

> "The Committee should recommend that statistical analyses be made on UFO data." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={38} q="The Committee should recommend that statistical analyses" />

Sagan also warned that sloppy case work — he cited a New Hampshire B-47 sighting
never checked against the aircraft's navigation logs — left "loop holes" for
critics "to cast doubt on the Air Force's credibility." That scientific instinct
sat uneasily beside the public-relations frame running through the rest of the
folder. In the 19 April 1966 implementation meeting, an official could write the
quiet part plainly:

> "Since the problem is 99% public relations, it is essential that the investigating teams have some modicum of skill in press relations." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={10} q="the problem is 99% public relations" />

The same memo frames the briefing of any future university teams as an exercise
in avoiding "pitfalls" so as "to give good Air Force public relations."
<Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={10} q="good Air Force public relations" /> The
tension is the story: a genuinely qualified panel, convened to answer a
public-image brief.

## Downstream: the Condon study and an early lean to the ET hypothesis

Secretary of the Air Force Harold Brown accepted the committee's recommendation
to "contract for a scientific team to investigate in depth certain selected
reported sightings of UFO's." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={18} q="scientific team to investigate in depth" /> That
contract went to the University of Colorado under Dr. Edward U. Condon.

The folder then does something the published Condon Report does not: it records
the Colorado team's private early thinking. A 16 May 1967 memo for the record,
based on verbatim notes of a Condon-team progress briefing, describes how the
group weighed competing hypotheses — and where its early sympathies lay. The best
working hypothesis that UFOs were real, the note says, was the one:

> "...proposed by Mr. Richard Hall, Deputy Director of NICAP." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={34} q="proposed by Mr. Richard Hall" />

Hall, the memo records, "definitely believes UFOs exist and are extra-terrestrial,
solid objects" <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={34} q="definitely believes UFOs exist" /> <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={34} q="extra-terrestrial, solid objects" /> — and, remarkably given the study's eventual
dismissive conclusions:

> "At this time, the UOC Committee apparently favors the Hall hypothesis." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={35} q="favors the Hall hypothesis" />

How that early lean toward an extraterrestrial hypothesis became the negative
final Condon Report is a separate story. What this folder documents is that the
lean existed, in the government's own contemporaneous notes.

The closing correspondence, from February 1969, confirms how the sponsors read
the outcome. Steiner sent committee members their personal copies of the Condon
Report with the assessment that it "puts the Air Force in an excellent position
to counter criticism" <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={26} q="excellent position to counter criticism" /> and
told O'Brien he believed "the Air Force got its money's worth."
<Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={27} q="Air Force got its money's worth" /> Orlansky,
returning his thanks, wrote that Condon's group "seem to have done a tremendous
job, just about what our committee thought was needed."
<Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={24} q="just about what our committee thought was" /> The
brief from 1966 — a prestigious report to get the Air Force off the hook — was,
by the sponsors' own account, delivered.

## The Robertson report, embedded again

This same folder carries something else worth flagging for readers of this
archive: a full embedded copy of the **1953 Robertson Panel report** (roughly
pages 44–77), the "Report of Meetings of Scientific Advisory Panel on
Unidentified Flying Objects." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={44} q="REPORT OF MEETINGS OF SCIENTIFIC ADVISORY PANEL" /> The
O'Brien committee explicitly reviewed it — the 3 February 1966 meeting statistics
list "Reviewed: Robertson Report, dated 17 January 1953." <Cite card="8c22358135a85824" page={7} q="Reviewed: Robertson Report, dated 17 January 1953" />

We do not re-litigate Robertson here. For that 1953 document — the order to
"strip the Unidentified Flying Objects of the special status they have been
given" — see the dedicated entry,
[The 1953 Robertson Panel](/finds/robertson-panel-1953-strip-special-status).
The one thing worth noting is continuity: the 1966 committee reached for the 1953
panel as its most recent precedent, and Release 4 now surfaces a fuller copy of
that report inside this later committee folder (card `8c22358135a85824`).

## What it is, and isn't

This is a policy and public-affairs record, not evidence of a craft, and it
should not be overread. The committee's members were real scientists doing real
work; Sagan's proposals for controlled experiments and statistical analysis are
exactly what a rigorous review should contain. Nothing here shows the Air Force
suppressing evidence of extraterrestrial visitation.

What the folder does show, plainly and in the government's own words, is the
frame in which the review was commissioned: a Director of Information conceding
663 unexplained reports from credible witnesses, and a public-affairs office
asking for a "prestigious scientific group" to produce "a position that will help
get the Air Force off the hook." The scientific study that followed was, to its
sponsors, an instrument of credibility management as much as inquiry — and the
same file preserves both that candid brief and the 1953 precedent it consciously
followed. Every quotation above is drawn from the released OCR text and pinned to
its page on card `8c22358135a85824` for verification.
