## What the document is

Pages 164–167 of FBI 62-HQ-83894 Section 2 (card `4844321219e306af`) are a typed copy of Kenneth Arnold's own written account of his June 24, 1947 sighting, signed at the bottom of page 167. The copy is attached to a 16 July 1947 memorandum from S/A Frank M. Brown of the 4th Air Force CIC documenting interviews with Boise-area aviation contacts about Arnold's reliability. <Cite card="4844321219e306af" page={168} q="Idaho Daily Statesman" />

A separate biographical statement ("Some Life Data on Kenneth Arnold") is on page 163. <Cite card="4844321219e306af" page={163} q="LIFE DATA" /> The sighting account that follows is what this finds entry is about.

## What Arnold says he was doing

Arnold opens by establishing that the day's flight was a marine search, not a UFO observation. On Tuesday, June 24, 1947, he had finished his work for Central Air Service at Chehalis, Washington, and took off about 2:00 p.m. for Yakima — but delayed an hour to look for a large marine transport reported down near the southwest side of Mt. Rainier. <Cite card="4844321219e306af" page={164} q="marine transport" />

He flew at approximately 9,500 feet, made one westward sweep of the high plateau, came back through a 360-degree turn over Mineral, Washington, climbed back to roughly 9,200 feet, and trimmed his airplane for Yakima. He notes the air was smooth that day, and that there was a DC-4 visible to his left and rear at approximately 14,000 feet. <Cite card="4844321219e306af" page={164} q="DC-4" />

Two or three minutes into that course a bright flash reflected on his airplane. He looked for the source of the reflection and saw nine objects flying north-to-south past Mt. Rainier.

## The procedural observations

Arnold's narrative does not describe the objects in dramatic terms. It describes what he did to estimate their parameters:

- **Distance.** He estimates his distance from the formation at twenty to twenty-five miles, with the angle almost at right angles to his course. <Cite card="4844321219e306af" page={165} q="twenty to twenty-five miles" />
- **Size, by parallax.** He compared the formation against the DC-4 to his left at the same time, using a Zeus fastener or cowling tool held up at arm's length. The objects appeared smaller than the DC-4 but spanned roughly as wide as the DC-4's outboard engines.
- **Speed, by stopwatch.** He timed the formation's transit between Mt. Rainier and Mt. Adams using his sweep second hand: one minute and forty-two seconds. <Cite card="4844321219e306af" page={165} q="forty-two seconds" />
- **Formation length, by ridge measurement.** He notes that as the lead object cleared a snow-covered ridge between the two mountains, the trailing object was just entering it on the other side. He measured the ridge at approximately five miles, and concludes the formation was at least that long.

He also notes what made him uneasy about the observation: as he watched the objects flip and flash in the sun, he could make out no tails on them, and "any pilot would justify more than a second look at such a plane." <Cite card="4844321219e306af" page={165} q="couldn't make out any tail" />

## The reflection-or-mirage rebuttal

The most methodologically interesting passage on the page is Arnold's response to skeptical hypotheses he had been offered between landing at Yakima and writing the statement. He identifies the source as "news men and experts":

> "A number of news men and experts suggested that I might have been seeing reflections or even a mirage. This I know to be absolutely false, as I observed these objects not only through the glass of my airplane but turned my airplane sideways where I could open my window and observe they with a completely unobstructed view. (Without sun glasses)" <Cite card="4844321219e306af" page={165} q="reflections or even a mirage" />

He is documenting that he had already test-falsified the reflection hypothesis at the time, in flight, by physically banking his airplane and opening the window — and that he had repeated the observation without intervening glass and without polarized lenses.

## What Arnold does not claim

The statement is striking for what it doesn't reach for. Arnold does not say what the objects were. He explicitly declines to: he writes that he has based the article on "positive facts" and that as far as guessing what he observed, it is "just as much a mystery to me as it is to the rest of the world." <Cite card="4844321219e306af" page={167} q="positive facts" />

He also notes — preserved in the FBI's copy — that despite openly inviting an investigation by the Army and the FBI as to the authenticity of his story or his physical and mental capabilities, he had received no interest from either Bureau. <Cite card="4844321219e306af" page={167} q="openly invited" /> The CIC field interview that the statement is attached to (S/A Frank M. Brown's memo of 16 July 1947) is, in effect, that follow-up — three weeks late, by way of an aviation editor at the *Idaho Daily Statesman*.

## Why this entry is in this archive

The Arnold sighting is the historical anchor of modern UFO reporting; the term "flying saucer" entered American English through press coverage of it. What's preserved in FBI 62-HQ-83894 Section 2 is something more specific than the cultural fact: it is Arnold's own typed account, in his own structure, with his own reasoning about what he saw and how he tried to falsify alternate explanations on the spot.

A reader interested in the Air Force's separate, contemporaneous filing of the same incident — Incident #17 on the standardized CHECK-LIST form, with a recorded "clocked speed" of approximately 150 MPH that contradicts the much higher figures often quoted in popular accounts — should compare the [DOW Box 7 incident summaries](/finds/dow-incidents-1-100). Same observation, same observer, two filing systems, two literary registers.
