## What the documents are

- **DOW-UAP-D154** (`e7f5382c5349c6e8`): *Transcript of a Presentation by Captain Edward J. Ruppelt, 1952*, 19 pages. Page 1 is a preface added by the releasing office. It describes the recording as "a March 1952 presentation by Captain Edward J. Ruppelt" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={1} q="a March 1952 presentation by Captain Edward J. Ruppelt" />. The preface is a modern summary. Nothing in it is Ruppelt speaking, and this entry quotes it only for the date.
- **DOW-UAP-PR160** (`208d494dece9b545`): *Presentation by Captain Edward J. Ruppelt, 1952*, the audio itself, about 65 minutes. This archive's machine transcript of the audio runs to five pages.

The transcript starts with microphone tests and ends "End of recording; nothing follows." Audience questions are marked "(Question from audience)". Two officers answer them. One is Ruppelt. The other is a senior officer who refers to him as "Lieutenant Ruppelt" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={16} q="Now, Lieutenant Ruppelt, in getting around on these cases" />. This entry attributes to Ruppelt only the main presentation and the answers where the context makes the speaker clear.

## The numbers

Ruppelt starts with the Air Force's 1949 conclusions (mass hysteria, hoaxes and "psychopathological people", misread conventional objects) and says the project has since dropped all but the last. Then he gives the figures:

> "Going back, we have approximately 800 cases now on record, and only about 2% of those are actual hoaxes or psychopathological people who just don't know what they see." <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={2} q="we have approximately 800 cases now on record, and only about 2% of those are actual hoaxes" />

> "We've gone through these reports and come up with the fact that about 20% are very good reports offered by very reliable people and are just absolutely no conclusions can be drawn." <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={2} q="about 20% are very good reports offered by very reliable people and are just absolutely no conclusions can be drawn." />

He repeats the figure near the end of the talk:

> "I'd say about 20% of the 800 or 900 reports that we now have just cannot be explained." <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={12} q="the 800 or 900 reports that we now have just cannot be explained." />

The machine transcript of the audio has the same sentence word for word <Cite card="208d494dece9b545" page={2} q="I'd say about 20% of the 800 or 900 reports that we now have just cannot be explained." />. In the Q&A the second officer breaks it down further: roughly 60% definitely identified, 20% that "maybe could have been a plane, a balloon, or a meteor", and "that 20% like the green fireballs and the lights that we just do not have an answer for" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={16} q="And then there's that 20% like the green fireballs and the lights that we just do not have an answer for." />.

## What he could not explain

**Silence.** Of the disc and sphere reports: "we have no knowledge of any type of power plant that would be completely silent" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={5} q="we have no knowledge of any type of power plant that would be completely silent." />. He comes back to it later: "on our good sightings, there's absolutely no noise ever reported" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={9} q="on our good sightings, there's absolutely no noise ever reported in" />.

**Where the reports cluster.** Describing a plot of U.S. sightings: "Around Los Alamos and the Albuquerque area, there's concentrations." He singles those out: "the ones around Oak Ridge and the ones around Albuquerque may possibly mean something, we don't know" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={3} q="But the ones around Oak Ridge and the ones around Albuquerque may possibly mean something, we don't know." />.

**Holloman.** He describes cinetheodolite crews at Holloman swinging off a missile launch to track several objects. He qualifies every number. The object "could possibly be a slow-moving daylight meteor" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={9} q="Uh, they don't know what it is, and it could possibly be a slow-moving" />. The crews were unsure all stations tracked the same object, but they did come up with an altitude reading of about 150,000 feet <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={9} q="of about 150,000 feet." />. On the speed:

> "They, they came up with a speed of about 3,500 miles an hour, but again, they won't back that up because they aren't, they aren't sure of the conditions that these readings were made under." <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={9} q="about 3,500 miles an hour, but again, they won't back that up because they aren't, they aren't sure" />

**Radar.** Air Defense Command radars had "tracked objects going anywhere from 1,000 up to about 3,500 miles an hour", and "We've never been able to determine, however, that these weren't malfunctions in the radar set" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={11} q="We've never been able to determine, however, that these weren't malfunctions in the radar set" />. His answer was instrumentation: radar-scope cameras, a long-focal-length camera slaved to the radar antenna, and diffraction-grating cameras for guards at Sandia and White Sands.

## Lubbock: the photographer who would not recant

The longest story in the talk is Lubbock, Texas, the previous late summer. Faculty members at Texas Tech saw formations of lights over several nights, and a college freshman photographed them. Ruppelt says the professors themselves thought the photographs were fake: "The college professors say that these pictures are faked" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={7} q="The college professors say that these pictures are faked." />. The Air Force tried to break the student's story. OSI interviewed him "about three times", and on the last occasion offered to let him "back out on this if you want to". He did not. He said he did not know what the lights were, only that he had taken the pictures, and:

> "he signed a sworn statement for us, so we had to drop it right there." <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={7} q="he signed a sworn statement for us, so we had to drop it right there." />

The ducks theory gets a fair hearing too. Many Lubbock residents said they saw ducks, and about half said the lights were not ducks. Ruppelt ends up "up against a blank wall on this certain, this one case" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={9} q="So we're up against a blank wall on this certain, this one case." />.

He also mentions a photo that is the subject of its own entry in this archive: "It was taken in Phoenix, Arizona, on the 7th of July, 1947" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={6} q="It was taken in Phoenix, Arizona, on the 7th of July, 1947." />. See [The Rhodes Photographs (Phoenix, 1947)](/finds/rhodes-photographs-phoenix-1947).

## The other half: no proof

The briefing is not a case for the extraordinary, and Ruppelt says so. On balloons, he criticizes his own service's past practice: "the Air Force has gotten into a lot of trouble in the past by very loosely tying up these balloons with the sightings" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={6} q="And the Air Force has gotten into a lot of trouble in the past by very loosely tying up these balloons with the sightings." />. He tells a Fort Monmouth case that looked like "this is it" and fell apart into a large balloon and flustered student radar operators: "Well, it turned out that the pilots had seen a big balloon" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={14} q="Well, it turned out that the pilots had seen a big balloon." />. And he states the project's evidentiary position:

> "Up till the present time, we've got no real proof that anything even exists." <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={12} q="till the present time, we've got no real proof that anything even exists." />

In his words the Air Force "can't back up any of these books by Scully or Keyhoe" <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={12} q="We can't back up any of these books by Scully or Keyhoe and say that" />. He sums up the posture as:

> "They may be from Mars, but if they are, we don't want to think they are. We don't want to think they're all balloons." <Cite card="e7f5382c5349c6e8" page={14} q="They may be from Mars, but if they are, we don't want to think they are. We don't want to think they're all balloons." />

## What we're not claiming

We are not claiming the "20%" is a count of anything extraordinary. By Ruppelt's own framing it is a count of good reports from reliable observers that the project could not explain with the data it had. He says in the same talk that it had "no real proof that anything even exists". The Holloman figures are ones the trackers themselves "won't back that up". The radar figures may be equipment faults, and he says so. The Lubbock photographs were never broken, and never shown to be genuine. The briefing's value is as a primary voice: the man running the Air Force project, on tape, giving his internal numbers and his doubts together. The transcript is an edited release, and the audio's machine transcript differs from it in places. We quote the edited transcript.
