## What this document is

Card `9ed140b9186c20c5` (**NASA-UAP-D008**) is an audio excerpt from the **Apollo 12 post-mission medical debriefing** — the tape labelled "Tape 12, 1969." Apollo 12 flew in November 1969, four months after Apollo 11, with **Charles "Pete" Conrad**, **Richard "Dick" Gordon**, and **Alan "Al" Bean** aboard. All three men are named on the tape ("Pete," "Dick," "Al"), which fixes the crew even where the recording does not fix the individual speaker.

For roughly eight minutes the excerpt is a single, continuous exchange about one thing: the light flashes. An interviewer opens by reading a phrase back to the crew from the written debriefing:

> "we read this paragraph in here about these, quote, coronal discharges that you guys were seeing, and that completely stops us." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="coronal discharges" />

"Coronal discharges" is the crew's-paperwork name for what the archive elsewhere calls the Apollo light flashes. The rest of the excerpt is the crew explaining, in unpolished first-person, what those flashes actually were.

A note on how this transcript was produced, because it governs every attribution below: unlike the Apollo 14 debriefs — which were **true two-channel** audio with the crew on one channel and the interviewers on the other — this Apollo 12 tape is a **single-channel** recording. The speaker labels here come from automated diarization guessing voices apart within one mixed track, and that guess is **not reliable**: it collapses three astronauts and at least one interviewer into two labels that drift. So this entry does not assign lines to individual astronauts, and it treats even the crew-versus-interviewer split as an inference from *content* (who is asking, who is describing) rather than a fact of the recording. Where the text is garbled the quotes below avoid it.

## "Not something external to you"

The single most important claim the crew makes is that the flashes are inside the observer. Asked how the effect behaves, a crew member is emphatic that it is one eye at a time, not both:

> "It's not a discharge that you see in both eyes. You're really seeing it with one eye or the other eye." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="It's not a discharge that you see in both eyes" />

and states the conclusion the whole exchange keeps circling:

> "it's a phenomenon that you're not seeing with both eyes. It's not something external to you, it's something that's internal." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="it's something that's internal" />

That is the same conclusion the Apollo 14 crew would reach, out loud, two years later — "definitely within the eye." Here it arrives faster and rougher, but it is the same finding: the flashes are not light in the cabin, they are events in the visual system.

## Two kinds: the flash and the streak

The crew had already, informally, built the taxonomy that the Apollo 14 session would refine. They distinguish two forms and even offer a mechanism for the difference:

> "We had 2 kinds. I guess the particle either strikes the eyeball perpendicular or it discharges parallel." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="the particle either strikes the eyeball perpendicular or it discharges parallel" />

> "we'd get either a flash like that or we'd get a streak." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="we'd get either a flash like that or we'd get a streak" />

The interviewer draws them out on the geometry. The streaks had a consistent orientation and travel — "the ones I saw were horizontal," <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={2} q="the ones I saw were horizontal" /> and one crew member had the impression a streak "went from right to left no matter which eye it was in." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={2} q="it went from right to left no matter which eye it was in" /> The point events, by contrast, were central and brief — "it was just like a pinpoint of light went and closed again," <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={2} q="it was just like a pinpoint of light went and closed again" /> which the interviewer paraphrases back as "like somebody was flashing a light at you." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={2} q="Like somebody was flashing a light at you" />

This is the streak-and-pinpoint vocabulary the Apollo 14 crew would later formalize into streaks, pinpoints, and novas — caught here at an earlier, blunter stage of the same descriptive project.

## An eye bias — this time to the left

There is a small, striking echo of the Apollo 14 record. The Apollo 14 session's strongest evidence was a **right-eye** bias the crew felt as a left-eye bias. In this Apollo 12 tape a crew member reports the opposite felt bias for himself:

> "I for some reason got them more with my left eye than my right eye, but I could tell which eye it was when I was concentrating on it." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="got them more with my left eye than my right eye" />

The Apollo 12 debrief has no tallied count to check that impression against — the systematic eye-count that made the Apollo 14 record so unusual does not exist here. What it has is the raw subjective report, offered with the honest hedge that it took concentration to localize the eye at all.

## The crew tests "external"

Like the Apollo 11 and Apollo 14 crews, the Apollo 12 crew reasoned their way past the obvious alternatives. They note that an earlier explanation on the table had been penetration of the spacecraft:

> "They thought it was something penetrating the spacecraft." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="They thought it was something penetrating the spacecraft" />

— and that the effect did not depend on the eyes being open or on darkness adaptation in any simple way: down in the dark sleeping station, "It wouldn't make any difference whether my eyes were open or closed." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={3} q="It wouldn't make any difference whether my eyes were open or closed" /> The crew also flags that the effect was cross-checked between all three of them — that what one man reported was "the exact phenomenon all 3 of us saw." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="What Pete described is the exact phenomenon all 3 of us saw" />

## The interviewer's half: a cosmic-ray search that predates Apollo

What makes this excerpt more than a third eyewitness account is the interviewer's side of it. The medical questioner is not hearing about this cold — he places it inside an existing physiological research program:

> "we've been looking for cosmic rays, you know, cosmic ray strikes and heavy particles, heavy nuclei that go through." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="we've been looking for cosmic rays" />

and dates that program:

> "we started clear back in Mercury" — taking photographs of the retina "to see if you could see streaks across the retina." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="we started clear back in Mercury" />

He even reasons about why the search had failed: the retina heals too fast to catch the damage after the fact, so "The only way you're ever going to see it is if you photograph it right after a particle goes by." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="The only way you're ever going to see it is if you photograph it right after a particle goes by" /> One crew member had earlier relayed that a correspondent told him this was, in any case, "a perfectly known phenomenon" — "You can get to across your eye from gamma rays or something." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="You can get to across your eye from gamma rays or something" /> The crew's own summary is blunter: "Apparently it's a completely known phenomenon." <Cite card="9ed140b9186c20c5" page={1} q="Apparently it's a completely known phenomenon" />

That is the historically interesting part. By November 1969 the flight-medicine side already had a cosmic-ray hypothesis and a retinal-imaging history reaching back to Mercury — and here it is being matched, in real time, against three astronauts who had just spent ten days watching the flashes happen.

## Where it sits in the arc

This archive can now show the Apollo light-flash phenomenon across four crews, and Apollo 12 is the piece that was missing from the middle. On the [Apollo 11 debriefing](/finds/apollo-11-debriefing) (July 1969) the crew is *discovering* the flashes — Aldrin noticing them with the lights out, Armstrong reasoning to "a neutron or some kind of an atomic particle." In this Apollo 12 debrief (November 1969) the *second* crew to fly confirms the effect independently, names it "coronal discharges," and states plainly that it is internal, not external. On the [Apollo 14 light-flash session](/finds/apollo-14-light-flashes) (1971) the crew and a medical team *measure* it — eye-bias tallies, coincidence timing, a dark-adaptation test — and conclude "definitely within the eye." On the [Apollo 17 debriefing](/finds/apollo-17) (1972) it is a formal experimental target, ALFMED. Apollo 12 is the second data point on that curve: no instrument, no tally, but a whole crew independently reproducing the observation and reaching the same internal-not-external conclusion, five months before Apollo 14 would nail it down.

## A note on method and its limits

Two cautions attach to everything above. First, the recording is single-channel, so the speaker labels are an automated guess and this entry deliberately keeps individual-astronaut attribution out of it — where the text says "a crew member," which of Conrad, Gordon, or Bean is speaking is not recoverable from the audio. Second, the transcript is a careful automated reading of an aged 1969 tape with real garbles in it (a few phrases are plainly mistranscribed and are not quoted here); a reader checking a quote against the audio should treat the transcript as a reading, not a canonical record.
