## What the document is

The relevant pages live in FBI 62-HQ-83894 Section 7 (card `f85532f0514320be`), pages 71–74. They are a Department of Defense Office of Public Information statement, dated to the early 1950s, written for use in answering public inquiries about Air Force investigations of unusual aerial phenomena. The cover bears the file enclosure stamp "62-83894-320 ENCLOSURE." <Cite card="f85532f0514320be" page={71} q="OFFICE OF PUBLIC INFORMATION" />

The statement narrates the project history familiar from Air Force public communications of the period: a Wright-Patterson study set up December 30, 1947; 375 reported sightings investigated through December 27, 1949; a transition to "normal intelligence function" at the Air Technical Intelligence Center thereafter. By the time of writing, "about 1500 reports" had been investigated.

The "Radar Sightings" section on page 73 is where the document does something a curated finds entry should pause on: it explains the *mechanism* the Air Force believes accounts for many radar contacts, and offers a concrete case as illustration.

## The mechanism the document is teaching

The statement opens its radar discussion with what reads as a position paper on a known atmospheric phenomenon. It states that some unusual radar images are reflections of ground objects from a layer of warm air above the earth — a temperature inversion. <Cite card="f85532f0514320be" page={73} q="temperature inversion" /> Such reflections, the document notes, can be as sharp as returns from an aircraft, with apparent speeds ranging from zero to "fantastic," and apparent motion in all directions. The institutional consequence is given in one sentence: such radar sightings have resulted in hundreds of "fruitless intercept efforts." <Cite card="f85532f0514320be" page={73} q="fruitless intercept efforts" />

This is an unusually direct statement for the period. The Air Force is acknowledging on the record, in a public-information document filed inside an FBI omnibus file, that hundreds of attempted intercepts of radar-only contacts had produced no closure on the supposed target.

## The Oak Ridge case as illustration

Immediately following that admission, the document offers a single illustrative case:

> "Bearing out the theory of temperature inversion reflection is an incident which occurred in January 1951 near Oakridge, Tennessee. Two Air Force aircraft attempted to intercept an unidentified 'object' and actually established a radar 'lock' on the object. Their altitude at the time was 7,000 feet. The unidentified object, according to their radar, appeared to be at an elevation of ten to 25 degrees. Three passes were made in an attempt to close on the object. In each instance the pilots reported that their radar led them first upward and then down toward a specific point on the ground." <Cite card="f85532f0514320be" page={73} q="radar 'lock'" />

What is documented is unambiguous. There were two aircraft. They obtained a radar lock — the document uses the word and puts it in scare quotes only on second mention. They flew three intercept passes. On each pass, the radar trace led them upward then back down toward the ground. The interpretation offered is that this geometry — a fixed point on the ground producing a return that only appears to climb and descend with the interceptor's own approach — is consistent with a temperature inversion bending the radar return from a stationary ground object.

No visual confirmation is recorded. The document does not say what was on the ground at the apparent termination point of the trace.

## What the document doesn't claim

The Oak Ridge paragraph is presented as supporting evidence for a mechanism, not as a debunking of the original incident. The paragraph does not say:

- That the pilots concluded at the time their target was a temperature inversion artifact.
- That a ground source was identified after the flights.
- That every subsequent radar-only interception in the file is similarly explained.

What it does say is that this geometry — climb-then-dive toward a ground point, repeated across three passes — is the geometry the Air Force was using as an internal training example for *why* radar-only contacts at unusual altitudes shouldn't always be taken at face value.

The same page goes on to acknowledge ionized clouds and thunderstorm activity as causes for some unidentified radar returns. The next page also clarifies that attempts at interception are "not made every time" an unidentified image briefly appears on a scope, because current Air Force interceptors require predictable target tracking to be effective. <Cite card="f85532f0514320be" page={74} q="not made every time" />

## Why the case is in this archive

The Oak Ridge incident does not survive in this file as a pilot's debriefing or an OSI investigative report; what survives is its use, four-plus years later, as a *teaching case* in an unclassified Air Force public-information statement. The entry is therefore methodologically valuable on two levels: it preserves a primary description of an actual three-pass intercept attempt at 7,000 feet against a radar contact, and it preserves the Air Force's own institutional reasoning about what that case is evidence *of*.

A reader is in a position to examine both layers. The radar lock and the climb-then-dive geometry are observed facts. The temperature-inversion attribution is the analytical frame the Air Force assigned. The two are separable.
