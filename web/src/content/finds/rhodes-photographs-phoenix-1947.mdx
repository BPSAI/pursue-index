## What the document is

FBI omnibus file 62-HQ-83894 Section 6 (card `13f86e95aed52840`) preserves a cluster of memoranda dated June 1952 about photographs taken by **Dr. William Albert Rhodes** of Phoenix, Arizona. The trigger for the 1952 memos was a phone call: Fred Blumenthal, in the office of syndicated columnist Drew Pearson, called Special Agent Wick on June 4, 1952, asking whether Rhodes was correct that the FBI had borrowed his negatives and refused to return them. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={180} q="borrowed his negatives" />

The Bureau's internal answer, drafted that same day for Mr. Tolson, retraces an institutional history that by then was already five years old.

## The original delivery

The Bureau's June 4, 1952 memo to Mr. Tolson reconstructs the original transaction. On the morning of August 30, 1947, Rhodes delivered the negatives to the Phoenix Office, where they were accepted with the understanding that they were being given to Mr. Fugate as a representative of Army Air Force Intelligence, and that there was — in the file's own phrase — "little, if any, chance" of Rhodes getting them back. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={182} q="little, if any, chance" />

The agent who collected them was **George Fugate, Jr.**, working under Colonel Donald Springer of A-2, Fourth Air Force, Hamilton Field — the same Springer whose cover memos appear elsewhere in this file, including the [Muroc affidavit transmittal](/finds/muroc-1947). The Phoenix Office assisted Fugate in talking with Rhodes, not in investigating the photographs themselves.

## The recurring custody question

The 1952 memos summarize an ongoing back-and-forth that the Bureau had conducted without resolution since 1949:

- **June 8, 1949** — OSI representative Lynn C. Aldrich, Phoenix, contacted the Phoenix Office because Rhodes had requested the negatives' return. Aldrich noted that Fugate's recollection of the matter was "hazy." <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={172} q="hazy" />
- **April 17, 1950** — Rhodes told the Phoenix Office that *True Magazine* would like the photographs. Phoenix referred him to OSI at Fourth Air Force Base, San Francisco, "since they had been made available to this agency by the Phoenix Office on August 30, 1947." <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={172} q="True Magazine" />

Each contact returns the same answer: the Phoenix Office gave the material to the Air Force in 1947, and the question of where the negatives now reside is somebody else's question.

## The June 1952 follow-up

The June 10, 1952 memorandum from W. A. Branigan to Mr. Belmont recommends the Bureau Liaison contact OSI directly — not to investigate the photographs but to determine whether OSI provided them to Pearson's office. The memo notes that Pearson, on his June 8 television program, demonstrated photographs of flying discs but says it is "not known" whether those are the same photographs as the ones in the captioned case. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={173} q="television program" />

Seven days later, V. P. Kear's June 17 follow-up memo records the result of the inquiry. Lieutenant Colonel Leroy Barnard and Lieutenant Colonel E. M. Neville of OSI were both contacted; both advised that no inquiries had been received by OSI from Blumenthal. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={174} q="no inquiries had been received" /> A separate inquiry to Air Force Intelligence A-2 (Colonel C. M. Young) confirmed that no inquiry had reached the Director of Intelligence either. The chain of custody, as far as the Bureau could reconstruct it, did not lead to Pearson.

## What the file establishes

The Rhodes photographs themselves are not in this section of the file. They are referenced only as having been transmitted in 1947 to Army Air Force Intelligence, with subsequent custody questions routed through OSI and A-2. What the section preserves is the institutional shape of the custody question itself — the back-and-forth between a witness who wanted his negatives back, a Phoenix Office that had passed them along five years earlier, and a Bureau that needed to answer Drew Pearson's office without making operational claims it could not source.

The Bureau's internal recommendations across the June 10 and June 17 memos are procedural: ask OSI, ask A-2, document the answers. There is no editorial framing of the underlying photographs as authentic, fraudulent, or significant. The file is doing institutional bookkeeping on physical objects whose disposition was, at the time of the memo, not fully resolved within the document itself.

For a reader, the value of the entry is not the original sighting; it is the archival record of how documentary control over flying-disc evidence worked across multiple agencies and years.
