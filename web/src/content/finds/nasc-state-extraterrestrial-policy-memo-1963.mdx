## What the document is

Card `aa3097b4c549a67a` is a **six-page memorandum from Maxwell W. Hunter II**, Member of the Professional Staff, **National Aeronautics and Space Council** (Executive Office of the President), to **Robert F. Packard**, Office of International Scientific Affairs, **U.S. Department of State**. It is dated **July 18, 1963**. The classification marking carried on pages 2 through 6 is **OFFICIAL USE ONLY** — not a national-security classification. The asset filename carried the date `7.18.1963`; the PURSUE manifest field `incident_date` had it recorded as `7/18/52`, a date mismatch that the document content does not support.

The original NARA-style filename token in the upstream listing — `59_64634_711.5612` — is consistent with a State Department record group 59 file under the 711.561x decimal classification.

## What the memo says

Hunter writes to Packard as a Space Council policy staffer asking State/OES to think about a problem that does not yet have a contingency framework: what diplomatic and scientific posture the United States should adopt if an extraterrestrial intelligence were ever encountered. He partitions the problem into three cases — non-technological solar-system natives, sub-light interstellar visitors, and faster-than-light visitors — and argues each implies a different posture and a different distribution of operational responsibility between scientific, military, and diplomatic offices.

His framing recommendation, on page 6: humanity should plan to "bury all Terrestrial hatchets" in advance of any such encounter. <Cite card="aa3097b4c549a67a" page={6} q="bury all Terrestrial hatchets" /> His central warning, on the same page, is that without contingency planning any actual encounter "would degenerate into grand panic." <Cite card="aa3097b4c549a67a" page={6} q="grand panic" />

The memo references prior internal deliberations on **Basic National Security Policy** (the BNSP planning process), specifically a "BNSP Task I" effort. The reference is brief — it does not summarize what BNSP Task I concluded, only that the topic was discussed there.

## Tone and provenance

The memo's tone is unusual for executive-office correspondence on letterhead. Hunter is dismissive about contemporary popular ufology while simultaneously arguing the U.S. government should plan for the contingency at a level above the sighting-investigation file. He notes on page 4 that the only existing literature he is aware of on the diplomatic problem is science fiction. The memo is signed by hand on page 6.

A stamp appears on page 1 indicating internal routing ("MRS. Date / BV"); no other markings appear.

## Why this card is in a UAP release

Most of the war.gov PURSUE corpus is sighting reports, MISREP forms, agency investigative files, and radar tracks. A small number of documents are policy or programmatic correspondence; the Hunter-Packard memo is one. Its inclusion in the release puts the executive branch on the record taking the contingency question seriously at the policy staff level in 1963 — a non-trivial historical claim that the document itself supports.

## About the named correspondents

Maxwell W. Hunter II was a real engineer-turned-policy-staffer; in later decades he became publicly associated with U.S. ballistic-missile-defense work. Robert F. Packard was an actual science-affairs officer at State during this period. The memo's surface — letterhead, signature block, addressee, classification handle — is internally consistent with a routine 1963 interagency communication and does not show signs of post-hoc fabrication.

## What the upstream did

Card `aa3097b4c549a67a` was present in the 2026-05-08 CSV with the asset URL `59_214434_sp_16_7.18.1963.pdf` — a filename that announces the document as a 7/18/1963 record. The CSV row also carried an `incident_date` field of `7/18/52`, which is internally inconsistent: the asset filename says 1963, the incident-date field says 1952. The agent-pass over the preserved OCR resolved the inconsistency: the document is the 1963 NASC-to-State Hunter-to-Packard memo described above.

The 2026-05-11 CSV no longer carries that asset URL. The same title pattern — `59_64634_711.5612[7-2852` — now points to a different file: `59_64634_711.5612[7-2852.pdf`. The upstream description on that row reads:

> "*a two page memorandum, dated July 28, 1952, relates to increased reports of unidentified flying objects.*" <Cite card="df43a479bc891969" page={1} q="a two page memorandum, dated July 28, 1952" />

The new file is two pages; the original was six. The new `incident_date` field is `7/28/52`; the original was `7/18/52`. The new asset filename string matches the title pattern (`711.5612[7-2852` ↔ `711.5612.[7-28-52]`); the original did not (its filename was `59_214434_sp_16_7.18.1963`). The new card_id is `df43a479bc891969`; the original was `aa3097b4c549a67a`.

What the upstream did, in concrete terms — and what the byte-level evidence later showed (see "Byte-level integrity finding" below) — is that they did two distinct operations on the listing in a single update: (a) they re-issued the 1963 Hunter-to-Packard memo at a corrected, 1963-coded title pattern, with bytes identical to the version present on 2026-05-08; and (b) they added a different 1952 State memorandum, whose filename and content match the original 1952-coded title pattern. All three card identities are preserved here:

- Original 2026-05-08 listing (1963 NASC memo, filed under a 1952-coded title): `/card/aa3097b4c549a67a` and `/pdf/aa3097b4c549a67a.pdf` (R2 mirror)
- Current 2026-05-11 re-listing of the same 1963 memo (corrected 1963-coded title, byte-identical content): `/card/9e2c2621d67dde12` and `/pdf/9e2c2621d67dde12.pdf` (R2 mirror)
- Current 2026-05-11 listing at the original 1952-coded title (a genuinely-new 1952 State memo, two pages): `/card/df43a479bc891969` and `/pdf/df43a479bc891969.pdf` (R2 mirror). OCR'd 2026-05-12.

## What the replacement document contains (OCR added 2026-05-12)

The replacement is a **two-page interagency memorandum dated July 28, 1952**, from "F.H." to a "Mr. Armstrong" at the State Department, subject "Flying Saucers." The cover line records that **Paul Nitze** asked the author what the Air Force thought about flying saucers and the author judged the question worth circulating at the morning meeting. The cover note credits the substantive content to a briefing the author received from **General Samford** at the IAC (Intelligence Advisory Committee). <Cite card="df43a479bc891969" page={1} q="Paul Nitze asked me" />

Major General John A. Samford was the USAF Director of Intelligence in 1952; his recorded position, per the memo, was that there is "very little that can be said to clarify the flying saucer business: It is still a complete enigma." <Cite card="df43a479bc891969" page={2} q="complete enigma" /> The memo then attributes the contemporary increase in observations to two factors: "vastly improved methods of observation and reporting, including not only radar but the reporting systems used by civilian and military pilots," and "the publicity which is given it, which takes on certain elements of a 'fad.'" <Cite card="df43a479bc891969" page={2} q="elements of a" />

Substantive historical claim in the memo: phenomena "falling within the general description of flying saucers have actually been known to exist and have been reported one way or another for over 100 years." Page 2 carries a `DECLASSIFIED NND 852931` stamp.

The two-page brief is consistent with the title-pattern's date and filename, lending weight to the metadata-correction reading of the swap. It does NOT, however, explain what mechanism *previously* filed the 1963 Hunter-to-Packard memo under this 1952-coded title pattern; that mis-filing is itself a curious artifact preserved by the original card.

The change is logged at [/removed](/removed) and the snapshot of the prior manifest is at `/data/snapshots/596cc1881aa97d2fa49a45edab14d60802616e73ce125d286120e00d967cafa2.json`.

## Byte-level integrity finding (added 2026-05-12)

When the 2026-05-12 archive-reconciliation pass retroactively pinned the three `/removed` preservation copies into the project's content-addressed byte-archive layer, the SHA-256 of card `aa3097b4c549a67a`'s preserved bytes was compared against every existing archive entry. The hash collided with an already-archived entry — the current-manifest card `9e2c2621d67dde12`. Both card identities resolve to the same R2 object: `archive/aba3ec3b8ef0240364308cf046ccbfcd252c5a5d7ef470d696b72c1f41b2502d.pdf` (1,598,931 bytes).

**What the byte-equivalence proves.** The upstream's 2026-05-11 change to the 1963 NASC memo's listing was a metadata-only operation on the content side. Every page, every signature, every stamp, every redaction in the document is bit-for-bit identical to the version captured on 2026-05-08. The 1963 content was not re-redacted, re-paginated, re-OCR'd, or otherwise altered. It was re-classified — given a corrected title and asset filename — without any modification to the underlying document bytes.

**What the byte-equivalence does NOT prove.** Upstream's *motivation* remains ambiguous. The byte-identical re-issue is consistent with a routine metadata correction (the 1963 document had been mis-filed under a 1952-coded title; cleaning that up). It is also consistent with a deliberate withdrawal-then-restoration carried out under cover of a metadata correction — for instance, to reset analytic continuity, break inbound links to the prior URL, or test whether downstream archives were watching. This entry continues not to assert which.

What the byte-equivalence *does* establish is the floor: regardless of motive, no editorial alteration of the 1963 document content accompanied the upstream change. Future upstream operations on this card can now be evaluated against a cryptographic baseline rather than a description-level one.
