## What the document is

Card `0f469fcf6fb42875` is the first Central Intelligence Agency document on the PURSUE index. Three OCR'd pages. Report number **FIRK-311/01638-77**, with distribution date **20 December 1977** and a Date of Information window of **November 1972 – November 1973**. Country covered: **USSR**. Acquired in **Germany**. The source is described on the cover sheet as a former Soviet citizen who served at the location reported on. <Cite card="0f469fcf6fb42875" page={1} q="former Soviet citizen" />

The character of the document is stated explicitly at the top of page 1, in bold, above the subject line:

> "THIS IS AN INFORMATION REPORT, NOT FINALLY EVALUATED INTELLIGENCE" <Cite card="0f469fcf6fb42875" page={1} q="NOT FINALLY EVALUATED INTELLIGENCE" />

That sentence is the document telling the reader what kind of intelligence object it is. An **Intelligence Information Report** (IIR) is a raw collection product — what a source said, transmitted from the field to the consumers in Washington, prior to evaluation, prior to corroboration, prior to integration into a finished analytical product. The header below it carries a **WARNING NOTICE — SENSITIVE INTELLIGENCE SOURCES AND METHODS INVOLVED** caveat. <Cite card="0f469fcf6fb42875" page={1} q="SENSITIVE INTELLIGENCE SOURCES AND METHODS" />

The subject of the report is **the Sary Shagan Weapons Testing Range**.

## What the report is mostly about

The page-1 summary catalogues, in order, the topics the source covered:

> "This report provides limited information on the Sary Shagan weapons testing range, to include facilities, work areas, security fencing, the regional headquarters (V/Ch 03080), and a warhead checkout unit (V/Ch 03142). Also included is limited information on the following: System-75 [SA-2] and System-300/Aldan [ABM-1 GALOSH] warheads; rumored laser research; and an unidentified aerial phenomenon. End of Summary." <Cite card="0f469fcf6fb42875" page={1} q="unidentified aerial phenomenon" />

The body sections preserved in pages 2–3 walk through warhead details: System-300/Aldan checked at Site 4 then taken to Site 35 for launching, identified by field comment as the ABM-1/GALOSH (paragraph 10); warhead dimensions, weight, and cassette layouts for the System-300/Aldan and System-75 (paragraph 11); a hearsay note on laser-weapon experiments involving powerful antennas (paragraph 13). <Cite card="0f469fcf6fb42875" page={2} q="ABM-1/GALOSH" />

The UAP observation is paragraph 14. It begins on page 2 and continues onto page 3.

## Section 14 — the observation itself

The source's account, as recorded by the reporting officer:

> "On one evening in late Summer 1973, Source observed an unidentified phenomenon at Site 7. While watching a sport competition between Canada and the USSR on television, he stepped outside for some air and observed an unidentified sharp (bright) green circular object or mass in the sky." <Cite card="0f469fcf6fb42875" page={3} q="bright" />

The geometry, as reported:

> "The object was situated west of the site at an angle of sighting of approximately 70 degrees. The altitude of the object was undeterminable." <Cite card="0f469fcf6fb42875" page={3} q="70" />

A field comment notes that the sky was cloudless that evening and that the source believed the green mass would have been higher than cloud level, but could not estimate the diameter. <Cite card="0f469fcf6fb42875" page={3} q="cloud level" />

The evolution of the phenomenon:

> "Within 10 to 15 seconds of observation, the green circle widened and within a brief period of time several green concentric circles formed around the mass. Within minutes the coloring disappeared. There was no sound, such as an explosion, associated with the phenomenon." <Cite card="0f469fcf6fb42875" page={3} q="concentric circles" />

A closing field comment:

> "Source had no opinion as to what this phenomenon was. There was no resultant rumors. Source could not provide any further details." <Cite card="0f469fcf6fb42875" page={3} q="no opinion" />

That is the entirety of the UAP section. One paragraph. One observation. One unrecalled date in late summer 1973, anchored to a televised Canada-USSR sporting event. No follow-up. No interpretation. No corroboration in the document itself.

## Why the embedding matters

The historical interest of this card is not the observation in isolation — a single secondhand account, recalled years after the fact, of a green circular mass with concentric rings, is too thin to support its own conclusions, and the document does not ask it to. The interest is **what it is sitting inside of**.

This is a CIA Directorate of Operations collection report from a former Soviet citizen who had walked the perimeter of the Sary Shagan range. The reporting officer collected detail on warhead diameters, cassette dimensions, ball-bearing counts in the System-75 fragmentation packages, the V/Ch military unit numbers for the regional headquarters and the warhead checkout unit. The same source, in the same debriefing, in the same numbered-paragraph sequence as the laser-research hearsay, mentioned an evening sighting that had stuck in his memory. The reporting officer wrote it down — verbatim enough that the OCR can recover the field comments without ambiguity — and the CIA distributed the report through the standard channels (NSA, DIA, STATE, FBI, NPIC and the rest are on the cover-sheet distribution list).

The page-1 summary did not bury the observation. It catalogued it in the same sentence as the warhead and laser-research material. The document treats the UAP report as **one of the source's deliverables**, neither inflated into a headline nor dropped from the summary.

That is the historical artifact: a single CIA-formatted line, in a 1977-distributed IIR on a Soviet ABM range, recording that a Soviet defector who served at Sary Shagan reported an unidentified aerial phenomenon as part of his standard debriefing. The phenomenon is not the subject of the report. It is **one entry in the source's catalogue of things he saw at the range**, transmitted with the same plumbing as the rest.

## How to read this document

A reader should hold three things at once:

1. **It is raw collection.** The "NOT FINALLY EVALUATED INTELLIGENCE" stamp at the top of page 1 is not boilerplate; it is the document telling its consumers what it is. An IIR carries what the source said. It does not certify whether the source was right, whether the phenomenon was correctly identified, whether the date was correctly recalled, or whether the observation has any explanation natural, technical, or otherwise.
2. **The DOI is November 1972 – November 1973; the distribution date is December 1977.** The source's recollection of "late summer 1973" was set down in a debriefing some years after the underlying event. Memory degrades; dates anchor to other dates (in this case, a televised sporting event). The temporal precision available from the document is "an evening in late summer 1973."
3. **The source had no theory.** The report records, on the face of it, that the source offered no opinion on what the phenomenon was and that "no resultant rumors" followed. The CIA reporting officer did not propose an interpretation either. The document is a primary-surface record of an observation, nothing more.

## What this is, and is not

This is not a CIA UAP study. The CIA has not, in this document, opened an investigation into the observation. The CIA has done what its Directorate of Operations does — collected what a foreign source said while debriefing him on a topic of operational interest, and transmitted the collection to consumers — and the observation happens to be on the list.

That is the editorial frame. The page-1 summary line — "and an unidentified aerial phenomenon" — is the single most informative phrase on the card, because it shows where in the intelligence cycle this kind of report can surface. It surfaces in collection. Whether anything was done with it after collection is not knowable from this document alone.
