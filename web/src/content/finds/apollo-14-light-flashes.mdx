## What these documents are

Two cards in the PURSUE archive hold the Apollo 14 technical crew debriefing, recorded in the Lunar Receiving Laboratory during the crew's post-mission quarantine in February 1971. Card `ffd9dfd4bceb163f` (**NASA-UAP-D026**) is the long medical debriefing — roughly an hour and a half of the flight surgeon's team and the crew working through exercise, diet, sleep, hydration, and vision. Card `69f1874d972fb44c` (**NASA-UAP-D027**) is the continuation.

For most of its length D026 is unremarkable aeromedical housekeeping. What makes the pair worth surfacing is what happens in the last few minutes of D026 and across nearly all of D027: a **light-flash session**, tacked onto the end of the debriefing almost as an afterthought, that turns into the most detailed first-person account of the Apollo light flashes anywhere in this archive.

A note on how these transcripts were produced, because it bears on every attribution below: both cards were transcribed from **true two-channel audio**, with the crew on one channel and the interviewers on the other. That partition — crew versus interviewer — is reliable, and it is the basis for every "the crew" and "the interviewers" label here. What the channel split does **not** support is telling the three astronauts apart. So this entry attributes statements to **the crew** collectively and to **the interviewers** collectively, and it does not assign individual astronaut names. (The one crew member ever named on the tape is Edgar D. Mitchell; the interviewers also refer to "Al" and "Stu," but per-speaker diarization inside the crew channel is not reliable enough to assert.)

## How the session got tacked on

The light-flash session is not a planned agenda item. It is relayed in near-real time from the crew office. An interviewer explains its origin:

> "Deke suggested that we should tack on a few questions about light flashes to the end of this briefing. That's what we're about to do." <Cite card="ffd9dfd4bceb163f" page={34} q="Deke suggested that we should tack on a few questions about light flashes" />

That is worth pausing on, because the surface of this exchange is exactly where an earlier, single-channel transcription of the same audio went wrong — it folded the interviewers and the crew together into a phantom composite speaker, and lines like the Slayton hand-off could read as if an astronaut were speaking. On the two-channel record the framing is unambiguous: **the questions come from the interviewers; the observations come from the crew.** The interviewers state the goal plainly — to "clarify the conditions under which you saw them." <Cite card="ffd9dfd4bceb163f" page={34} q="clarify the conditions under which you saw them" />

## "Definitely within the eye"

The single most important line in the session is also its shortest. Near the end, an interviewer asks the question the whole exercise was built to answer:

> "Did you get the impression the light flashes could have been within the cabin, or definitely within your eye?" <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={7} q="within the cabin, or definitely within your eye" />

The crew's answer:

> "Definitely within the eye." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={7} q="Definitely within the eye" />

Everything else in the session is, in effect, the crew and the interviewers assembling the evidence that makes that four-word answer defensible. The remarkable thing about the Apollo 14 record is that they do the assembling out loud.

## The right-eye bias the crew could not feel

The strongest evidence in the session is a mismatch between what the crew subjectively believed and what the tapes objectively recorded. Asked whether they could reliably tell which eye a flash appeared in, the crew reports a felt bias:

> "I would have sworn that I was biased to the left." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={5} q="I would have sworn that I was biased to the left" />

The interviewers, working from the tallied in-flight reports, had the opposite:

> "You had 12 right and 6 left. And Al, you had 10 right and 4 left... It was all very heavily biased to the right eye." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={5} q="12 right and 6 left" />

The subjective impression and the counted data point in opposite directions — a crew member who "would have sworn" he favored the left eye was, on the record, running better than two-to-one toward the **right**. The interviewers draw the methodological moral explicitly later in the session: "your subjective impression is not necessarily right... so you need to get the data real time." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={13} q="subjective impression is not necessarily right" /> This is the passage that most distinguishes the Apollo 14 record from the Apollo 11 one: it is not just an account of a phenomenon, it is an account of a phenomenon being measured against its own witnesses.

## Coincident flashes

A second strand of objective evidence: flashes reported by two different crew members at the same instant. The interviewers had gone back through the voice tapes and found them:

> "There were 3, from listening to the tapes, 3 events, and they occurred in cases where different pairs were involved, which were coincidences." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={6} q="different pairs were involved, which were coincidences" />

The crew confirms the timing was tight enough to be meaningful — the second man's flash came "certainly within a fraction of a second" of the first man's callout. <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={6} q="within a fraction of a second" /> Coincident events across separate observers are hard to explain as independent quirks of three tired retinas and easy to explain as a shared external flux passing through the cabin — which is the direction the whole session is pushing.

## The flashlight that would not kill it

The crew ran an in-flight test that reads like a deliberate control. One crew member shined a flashlight into his own eyes to see whether destroying his dark adaptation would suppress the flashes. It did not:

> "putting the flashlight in my eyes did not seem to destroy my dark adaptation." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={11} q="did not seem to destroy my dark adaptation" />

> "they didn't diminish in brightness when I did the flashlight across my eyes. They were still just as bright 30 seconds later as they had been before I used the flashlight." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={13} q="didn't diminish in brightness" />

The interviewers connect the observation to the mechanism they suspect. If the flashes survive a flashlight to the eye, and if — as one crew member also reports — "you do not have to be dark adapted to see these," <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={12} q="You do not have to be dark adapted to see these" /> then the flashes are probably not ordinary light entering the eye at all:

> "it almost certainly proves that it's not a real light generated in your eye. It's actually an interaction." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={12} q="not a real light generated in your eye" />

There is a residual puzzle they flag honestly: at the start of the formal observation period it took **17 minutes** before anyone saw a flash — "17 minutes, and no response from you" <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={12} q="17 minutes, and no response from you" /> — yet the flashlight test produced flashes within 30 to 40 seconds. The crew and interviewers turn that discrepancy over without forcing a conclusion, floating a threshold or attention effect and leaving it open.

## A taxonomy of flashes

Much of the session is the interviewers, with the room darkened and some kind of projector, trying to get the crew to pin down the *shape* of what they saw. The crew converges on a small vocabulary. There is the **streak** — the sharp, unmistakable one: "There was no mistaking what it was." <Cite card="ffd9dfd4bceb163f" page={34} q="There was no mistaking what it was" /> Both a "single streak and the double shot" appeared clearly. <Cite card="ffd9dfd4bceb163f" page={34} q="the single streak and the double shot" /> Streaks tended to sit "on the periphery" of the visual field. <Cite card="ffd9dfd4bceb163f" page={35} q="appeared to be on the periphery" /> Some seemed to have direction, which the crew is careful to hedge — the impression of motion may have been "a ball of light moving in a direction, leaving a tail," reconstructed after the fact rather than seen. <Cite card="ffd9dfd4bceb163f" page={35} q="a ball of light moving in a direction, leaving a tail" />

Then the rarer forms, catalogued in D027. The **pinpoint**: "a pinpoint of light for an instant." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={2} q="a pinpoint of light for an instant" /> And the **nova** (one crew member also says *supernova*) — "the nova as we call them" <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={3} q="the nova as we call them" /> — "more of a blob that seemed to start" small and expand, diffuse and bright. <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={4} q="more of a blob that seemed to start" /> The crew works to keep the terms honest, noting that individuals used *flash*, *star*, and *nova* differently, and settling for one crew member on "the pinpoint versus an explosion" as the only distinction he trusted. <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={4} q="the pinpoint versus an explosion" />

## The two surprises

Two observations sit outside the streak-and-nova taxonomy, and the interviewers flag one of them as new: a **diffuse** glow, unlike any of the point or line events. An interviewer calls it "a new phenomenon which we hadn't expected" <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={9} q="a new phenomenon which we hadn't expected" /> and asks the crew to "describe this lightning behind the cloud." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={9} q="lightning behind the cloud" /> The crew reaches for a childhood analogy — "just what we used to call heat lightning as a kid" <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={9} q="heat lightning as a kid" /> — but corrects the analogy in the same breath: unlike real heat lightning there was no streak, it was "just a diffuse lighting." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={9} q="just a diffuse lighting" />

The second is a single anomalous flash of color. Nearly everything the crew saw was white or silver; the interviewers ask, "Did anyone else see color?" <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={14} q="Did anyone else see color" /> The one exception, attributed on the tape to Mitchell, the crew lands on describing as a "blue diamond." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={14} q="Like a blue diamond" /> It was not, they agree, the brightest thing they saw — just the only colored one.

## What it predicts

The session closes by looking forward. The crew's own recommendation is methodological — that a future crew should "take a definite period, set it aside" for the observation rather than catch flashes at random <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={10} q="take a definite period" /> — and the interviewers predict, correctly, that the phenomenon has earned a place on the schedule:

> "the pressure on the subject which has built up is such that there will be some formal experiment on 15." <Cite card="69f1874d972fb44c" page={15} q="there will be some formal experiment on 15" />

They were early on the mission number but right on the substance. A formal, instrumented light-flash experiment — **ALFMED**, the Apollo Light Flash Moving Emulsion Detector — flew on Apollo 16 and Apollo 17, sandwiching a dark-adaptation emulsion detector around an astronaut's head to correlate seen flashes with the tracks of individual cosmic-ray nuclei.

That places the Apollo 14 session precisely in the middle of an arc this archive can now show end to end. On the [Apollo 11 debriefing](/finds/apollo-11-debriefing), in 1969, the crew is *discovering* the flashes — Aldrin noticing them with the lights out, Armstrong reasoning his way to "a neutron or some kind of an atomic particle" with no formal vocabulary for it. On the [Apollo 17 debriefing](/finds/apollo-17), in 1972, Schmitt is *characterizing* them around a dedicated experiment. Apollo 14, in 1971, is the hinge: no instrument yet, but a crew and a medical team running the phenomenon through real controls — coincidence timing, eye-bias tallies, a dark-adaptation test — and reaching, out loud and on the record, the conclusion the instrument would later confirm.

## A note on method and its limits

Everything above rests on the crew-versus-interviewer channel split, which is reliable. Three cautions attach to it. First, the individual astronaut identities inside the crew channel are **not** asserted here — where the text says "the crew," a specific one of the three men is usually speaking, but which one is not recoverable from the channel data. Second, after roughly the first five minutes the crew moved to a shared boom microphone, so both channels pick up the room; the tightened transcript prunes the resulting cross-talk echoes, and a few boom-mic passages remain harder to attribute cleanly than the timestamps might suggest. Third, the transcript corrects only high-confidence mishearings and marks genuine uncertainty in place; readers who want to check a quote against the audio should treat the transcript as a careful reading, not a canonical one.
