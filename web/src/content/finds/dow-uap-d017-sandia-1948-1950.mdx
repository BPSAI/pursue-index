## What the document is

DOW-UAP-D017 (card `09001c140b97a5db`) is 116 pages of general correspondence drawn from the classified documents library of the **Armed Forces Special Weapons Project (AFSWP)** at Sandia Base, Albuquerque. AFSWP was the post-1947 successor organisation to the Manhattan Engineer District — the joint Army-Navy command responsible for the U.S. nuclear stockpile. The file collects intra-agency cables, OSI summaries, weather observations, particle-collection laboratory reports, and the minutes of a February 1949 Los Alamos conference, all relating to unidentified aerial phenomena observed over and around the New Mexico nuclear-weapons complex between December 1948 and May 1950.

The institutional weight of the file is what distinguishes it. This is not a hobbyist collection or a public-relations dossier. It is the operating file of the agency holding custody of the U.S. nuclear stockpile, treating recurrent aerial phenomena near its installations as a matter requiring scientific attention. <Cite card="09001c140b97a5db" page={21} q="continued occurrence" />

For the narrower scientific framing — Lincoln LaPaz's eleven catalogued differences from meteor behaviour — see the companion entry on the [LaPaz green-fireball reports](/finds/lapaz-green-fireballs-1948-1950). The present entry concerns the institutional record around those events: who else was reading LaPaz, what they were doing about it, and what physical evidence they were attempting to collect.

## The 209-sighting tabulation

Pages 23–60 are a CONFIDENTIAL columnar log: *Summary of Sightings of Unknown Aerial Phenomena, 17th District OSI*. The transmittal cover memo of 25 May 1950 from Lt. Col. Doyle Rees explains that the 17th District had assumed responsibility for collecting and reporting these observations since December 1948 at the direction of a multi-agency liaison meeting that included Fourth Army, AFSWP, the FBI, the Atomic Energy Commission, the University of California, the Air Force Scientific Advisory Board, AMC, and OSI. <Cite card="09001c140b97a5db" page={20} q="liaison meeting" />

Each row records date, time, number and reliability of observers, general area, apparent direction of flight, apparent altitude, course (horizontal-to-vertical), colour, train, sound, shape, apparent size, apparent speed, and manner of disappearance. Each row terminates with an evaluation key: **(1)** Green Fireball Phenomena, **(2)** Disk or Variation, **(3)** Probable Meteor. The numbered observations run from January 1948 to May 1950 and reach #209 at the close of the table.

Geographic coverage is dense and concentric on the weapons complex: Albuquerque, Sandia Base, Los Alamos, Kirtland AFB, Holloman AFB, Alamogordo, Tularosa, Roswell, Vaughn, Las Vegas (NM), Datil, Window Rock (AZ), El Paso, Camp Hood (TX), Farmington, and others. The reliability column is dominated by "R" (Reliable) entries, with many observers identified as Los Alamos security inspectors, military and commercial pilots, and OSI special agents.

## Three incidents from the table

The catalogue's entries are terse. A representative cross-section:

- **Sighting #179, Los Alamos, 24 February 1950.** A single reliable observer reported an aluminium-coloured spherical object, "rather large," circling at considerable altitude before departing to the east between 1315 and 1400 hours. The phenomenon was evaluated as Green Fireball / Disk. <Cite card="09001c140b97a5db" page={55} q="Circled" />
- **Sightings #191–192, Farmington, New Mexico, 16 March 1950.** Ten unknown-reliability observers reported a daylight formation of bright, luminous, tin-foil-like discs "turning on their axes and maneuvering up and down," from sizes ranging from one inch to six inches at arm's length, faster than conventional aircraft, in two distinct observations within an hour of each other. Both were evaluated as Disk or Variation. <Cite card="09001c140b97a5db" page={57} q="turned on their axis" />
- **Sighting #200, Sandia Base, 21 March 1950.** A single unknown-reliability observer reported two silver round objects, dime-sized at arm's length, gradually fading from view at "excessive jet flight" speed about 45° above the horizon. Evaluated as Disk or Variation. <Cite card="09001c140b97a5db" page={59} q="2 objects" />

These are unremarkable rows within the table. They are unremarkable specifically because the table contains two hundred-odd of them.

## The Mandelkorn trip report (16 February 1949) — and Project Grudge

The most consequential single document in the file is a four-page trip report dated 18 February 1949 by Commander Richard S. Mandelkorn, U.S.N., of AFSWP's Research and Development Division, Sandia Base. The report — Series B, Copy 1 in the Sandia Security and Intelligence Division — minutes a 16 February 1949 conference at Los Alamos convened to consider the green-fireball phenomena.

The attendee list is the most striking feature of the document. For Los Alamos: **Norris E. Bradbury** (Director, Los Alamos), **Marshall Holloway**, **Fred Reines**, **John Manley**, **Edward Teller**, Elmo Morgan (AEC), Sidney Neuberger (Security), Maxwell (AFSS), and Hoyt. For Fourth Army: Major William A. Godsoe and Major Wynn. For USAF: Captain Neef. For the University of New Mexico: **Dr. Lincoln LaPaz**. For Sandia: Commander Mandelkorn. <Cite card="09001c140b97a5db" page={90} q="Bradbury" />

Captain Neef opened the meeting by stating that "this question had been classified military SECRET under the name, Project 'Grudge', and that the investigation was now the primary responsibility of USAF, Air Materiel Command, T-2." <Cite card="09001c140b97a5db" page={90} q="Project &quot;Grudge&quot;" /> This is one of the earliest extant institutional references to Project Grudge in U.S. government holdings.

Teller's contribution is recorded in unusual detail. He calculated that an object moving at the observed velocity (~8 miles per second) at the observed altitude (~8 to 10 miles) and emitting the observed light output (~10¹⁴ ergs per second) would have to have a mass of roughly twenty grams — and would have produced a shock-wave noise easily audible ten kilometres from the source. No sound had been observed. Teller's tentative opinion, recorded by Mandelkorn, was that the green fireballs were "not material objects passing through the air" and that an explanation should be sought in "electronics and optics" rather than hydrodynamics. <Cite card="09001c140b97a5db" page={92} q="not material objects" /> Bradbury demurred on the electronics hypothesis, observing that it would raise its own intractable problems.

Mandelkorn's conclusion, written for distribution to Fourth Army G-2 and USAF, states: "It is my belief that these phenomena, particularly if there are any further incidents, are deserving of serious consideration until their source and meaning have been satisfactorily explained… there is cause for concern of the continued occurrences of unexplainable phenomena of this nature in the vicinity of sensitive installations." <Cite card="09001c140b97a5db" page={93} q="sensitive installations" />

## The Crozier copper-particle collections

The file's most concrete physical-trace evidence is W. D. Crozier and Ben K. Seely's 10 August 1949 report from the New Mexico School of Mines, *An Attempt to Collect Airborne Particles Associated with the Fireball of July 24, 1949*. Aerosol-impactment equipment processing 34 litres of air per minute, with rubeanic acid (dithiooxamide) applied to identify copper compounds, was used to make systematic collections at the School of Mines campus starting thirteen and a half hours after a fireball reportedly passed over Socorro at 8:26 p.m. on 24 July 1949.

The collections registered positive copper indications, with particle counts in the smallest size range (1–15 microns) rising sharply some 35 hours after the fireball event. <Cite card="09001c140b97a5db" page={6} q="copper-bearing particles" /> Crozier qualified the result heavily: "it seems very hazardous to draw any definite conclusion associating the copper-bearing particles collected with the fireball of July 24." But he noted the residuum of possibility and recommended continued airborne collection using equipment already on hand and proved in flight.

A subsequent collecting trip (10 August 1949) covering nine locations on Highway 84 north of Highway 66 produced a single anomalous sample, R-104L, containing a large number of copper-bearing particles in a granular matrix unfamiliar to the laboratory. The collection taken immediately afterward at the same location showed practically no copper. Crozier's tentative conclusion: "no widespread significant dispersion of copper-bearing particles was present in the atmosphere in the region covered at the time the collections were made." <Cite card="09001c140b97a5db" page={4} q="copper-bearing particles" /> The copper claim is therefore not a confident affirmative finding. It is a documented attempt by a working aerosol laboratory to falsify, and it produced one anomalous sample its authors could not source.

The relevance for the broader file: LaPaz's Seventh Report noted that laboratory tests had identified the green fireballs' colour as consistent with copper-salt emission in a blowpipe flame, and the Crozier collections are the corresponding attempt to recover physical copper material from the air mass through which a fireball had passed.

## What the file establishes

DOW-UAP-D017 does not establish that the green fireballs, the discs, or the orbs observed near Sandia were exotic. It establishes a different set of facts:

- Between December 1948 and May 1950, the 17th District OSI catalogued 209 reliable-to-unknown-reliability sightings of unidentified aerial phenomena concentrated within and immediately around the New Mexico nuclear-weapons complex.
- The agency holding institutional custody of the U.S. nuclear stockpile — AFSWP, the Manhattan Project's direct organisational successor — kept the resulting paper trail in its own classified documents library, distributed Mandelkorn's trip report to its own Security and Intelligence Division, and was a formal participant in the multi-agency liaison structure responsible for the investigation.
- On 16 February 1949, the Director of Los Alamos, three Manhattan Project veterans (Teller, Manley, Holloway), the discoverer of the neutrino (Reines), the Institute of Meteoritics director (LaPaz), AEC and security representatives, and Fourth Army and USAF intelligence officers sat in the same room at Los Alamos under the codename Project Grudge to consider what the phenomena were. Teller produced a back-of-envelope calculation on the spot.
- A working aerosol laboratory at the New Mexico School of Mines was contracted to look for copper-bearing particles in the wake of fireball passes. The collections produced one anomalous sample its principal investigator declined to characterise.

What the file does not establish is what the phenomena were. Mandelkorn explicitly leaves that question open. Teller leaves it open. LaPaz leaves it open. The 17th District leaves it open and recommends continued scientific study. The reader's takeaway is the institutional shape: the U.S. nuclear establishment, two years removed from Hiroshima, treated 209 recurrent aerial observations near its custody installations as a matter for documented work product — and Edward Teller treated it as worth a calculation.
