## What the document is

**DOW-UAP-D101** (`d973d98ce1f1f852`) is a seven-page Intelligence Information Report. The Department of War catalog describes it as a Gulf of Oman report from 2021. The collector line reads "AFSOC A2OX - MOTHERSHIP" and the report is dated 27 April 2022, about seven months after the events it describes <Cite card="d973d98ce1f1f852" page={1} q="AFSOC A2OX - MOTHERSHIP" />. Every page carries a declassification stamp by the USCENTCOM Chief of Staff dated 20 March 2026.

It is not an assessment. The report's own banner reads:

> "INFORMATION REPORT, NOT FINALLY EVALUATED INTELLIGENCE" <Cite card="d973d98ce1f1f852" page={4} q="INFORMATION REPORT, NOT FINALLY EVALUATED INTELLIGENCE" />

That means the report relays what a source said. It does not say the source was right. Everything below is the crew's account as written down by an intelligence collector, and nothing in the report evaluates it.

Six video clips are listed as an attachment: a PowerPoint "containg six" [sic] embedded videos <Cite card="d973d98ce1f1f852" page={6} q="Powerpoint containg six" />. The release also includes video cards in the Release 5 set; they have no text layer, so nothing here quotes them.

## The account, in the report's order

The report's summary says that the crew observed "approximately 25 instances of UAPs" during a live-fire exercise, seen as cold orbs flying at lower altitudes between 250 and 1,300 MPH <Cite card="d973d98ce1f1f852" page={1} q="approximately 25 instances of UAPs" />. It then adds that the objects "also appeared to react to the aircraft firing its main" cannon <Cite card="d973d98ce1f1f852" page={1} q="UAPs also appeared to react to the aircraft firing its main" />.

The detail is on page 2. The aircraft was in an orbit around a water-activated flare it had dropped as a mock target. The Combat Systems Officer was scanning with the aircraft's electro-optical/infrared sensor. When the officer slewed back to the flare, the report says, they "observed two approximately four-foot diameter cold orbs" held stationary over the flare for about 15 seconds <Cite card="d973d98ce1f1f852" page={2} q="they observed two approximately four-foot diameter cold orbs" />. Then the trigger was pulled for the 105mm cannon. The report continues that, during the delay between trigger pull and recoil, the officer saw the pair of objects

> "rapidly fly away from the flare and out of the sensor feed without changing" altitude. <Cite card="d973d98ce1f1f852" page={2} q="UAPs rapidly fly away from the flare and out of the sensor feed without changing" />

Over the rest of the sortie the crew reports seeing more. Two objects flew in a loose, coordinated formation, rising and falling like a wave, "in a manner similar to how dolphins playfully swim in a pod" <Cite card="d973d98ce1f1f852" page={2} q="manner similar to how dolphins playfully swim in a pod." />. A source comment in parentheses says the objects knew they were flying together. That is the source's characterization, not an observation of the sensor.

## The speed figures

The report says the Combat Systems Officer worked out the speeds using the aircraft's targeting tool and "clocked their maximum observed maneuvering speed at 1300 miles per" hour, and a minimum of 250 <Cite card="d973d98ce1f1f852" page={2} q="aircraft's TACTOOL and clocked their maximum observed maneuvering speed at 1300 miles per" />. It then states "No means of" propulsion were observed <Cite card="d973d98ce1f1f852" page={2} q="No means of" />.

The report does not say how a speed was derived from a single infrared sensor feed. A speed computed from a sensor image needs a distance, and the report does not give one. We cannot tell from the document whether the figure assumes a range, so we do not treat 1,300 mph as established.

## The recorder

The crew tried to save the full infrared footage. The report says the aircraft's digital video recorder "inexplicably failed and the video data became corrupted" <Cite card="d973d98ce1f1f852" page={2} q="Recorder (DVR) inexplicably failed and the video data became corrupted." />. What survives is the six-clip attachment described above, captured after the fact. That is why the most useful material in this report is a reporter's narrative of what the clips show. It cannot be checked against the full footage in the file.

## How it compares to other modern reports

This is an intelligence information report, a different form from the mission reports (MISREPs) in the Department of War series. Compare how those read in [How the Modern Military Writes Down a UAP Encounter (Syria, 2024)](/finds/dow-d32-syria-october-2024) and [the Arabian Gulf report of October 2023](/finds/dow-d23-arabian-gulf-october-2023). For another case where aircrews describe objects whose performance they could not explain, see [the Navy range debriefs of 2019 to 2020](/finds/navy-range-fouler-debriefs-2019-2020). For the broader question of what ordinary things look like in infrared, see [What's Not UAP](/finds/whats-not-uap).

## What we're not claiming

We are not claiming these were anything unusual. The report is an unevaluated relay of a source's account, not a finding. The reaction to the cannon is the crew's reading of timing: a short delay between a trigger pull and a recoil, seen on a sensor feed, with no reference given for how long the delay was. The "cold orb" description is a sensor-mode description, and the report does not say what an object of that appearance would be in a visible-light image. Small objects close to the water and near a flare could have ordinary explanations that a sensor feed alone cannot rule out, and we cannot rule them in or out. The document is heavily redacted; the personnel, the unit, and the location are not given, and we do not infer them.
