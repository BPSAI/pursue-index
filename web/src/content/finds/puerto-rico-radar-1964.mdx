## What the documents are

Release 5 carries two CIA records of one set of events near Puerto Rico in November 1964.

- **CIA-UAP-D022** (`7b2fec96cf493ed7`): six pages, titled in the catalog as an unidentified flying object reported near Puerto Rico. It is a TOP SECRET file in the BYEMAN control system, with an OXCART project marking on its covers. It holds two memoranda. The first is from the Assistant Director for Special Activities, Colonel Jack C. Ledford, dated 1 February 1965. The second is the reply from the Assistant Director for Scientific Intelligence, Donald F. Chamberlain, dated 11 February 1965.
- **CIA-UAP-D023** (`64b4b5770a796616`): two pages, SECRET "Briefing Notes for Mr. Walter N. Elder", undated.

Both men are named in their official roles. The released pages mark Ledford's and Chamberlain's offices, and no private person appears.

## The Navy's account

The briefing notes are the plainest statement of what the Navy reported. On 19 November the USS Gyatt "picked up on its radar a contact tracking in excess of 3,800 knots", while working a Navy F-8 Crusader at 35,000 feet <Cite card="64b4b5770a796616" page={1} q="contact tracking in excess of 3,800 knots." />. The object slowed and steadied about nine miles from the fighter, and when the pilot tried to close it, the notes say it

> "accelerated at extremely high speed in a 20° climb and pulled away from the" fighter rapidly. <Cite card="64b4b5770a796616" page={1} q="ated at extremely high speed in a 20° climb and pulled away from the" />

The notes add a second report five days later, on 24 November, when the range again "held a high-speed, high-altitude target" that pulled away from another Crusader <Cite card="64b4b5770a796616" page={1} q="held a high-speed, high-altitude target which simultaneously pulled" />. They say the Crusader pilot saw the object "in full moonlight" and identified it as a delta-shaped object about the size of a jet fighter <Cite card="64b4b5770a796616" page={1} q="in full moonlight, weather was good and visibility was good." />. The Gyatt confirmed the radar tracks "with motion pictures of radar scope presentation" <Cite card="64b4b5770a796616" page={2} q="confirmed radar tracks with motion" />.

## The OXCART memo: was it our aircraft?

The first memo in D022 is the one that explains why a CIA program office was involved. Ledford writes that his office and the scientific intelligence office "received similar information regarding a UFO in the vicinity of Puerto Rico" <Cite card="7b2fec96cf493ed7" page={6} q="regarding a UFO in the vicinity of Puerto Rico during period" />. He relays that a Navy cable had told the Atlantic Fleet that the object was identified by "CO Ramey" as a YF-12A, the Lockheed interceptor related to the A-12 that the program flew. And then:

> "We think ONI finally convinced, through our" efforts, that UFO in fact not YF-12A or similar category <Cite card="7b2fec96cf493ed7" page={6} q="We think ONI finally convinced, through our" />

In plain terms, the office that ran the reconnaissance aircraft says it worked to persuade Navy intelligence that the object was not one of its aircraft. It asks the scientific intelligence office to analyze the original radar film to "determine if any further information can be gotten" through scientific analysis <Cite card="7b2fec96cf493ed7" page={6} q="determine if any further information can be gotten" />.

## The analysis

Chamberlain's reply of 11 February 1965 reaches a narrower conclusion than the headline speed. After analyzing the radar photography, it says there was, in fact, "no high altitude ultra-high-speed UFO north of Puerto Rico" during the period <Cite card="7b2fec96cf493ed7" page={3} q="no high altitude ultra-high-speed UFO north of Puerto Rico" />. It still accepts that there were "apparently valid tracks and/or observations of unidentified aircraft" in the area on five dates <Cite card="7b2fec96cf493ed7" page={3} q="apparently valid tracks and/or observations of unidentified aircraft" />.

The memo repeats the Navy's number, "The USS Gyatt reported a radar track in excess of 3800 knots." <Cite card="7b2fec96cf493ed7" page={3} q="The USS Gyatt reported a radar track in excess of 3800 knots." /> But its own reading of the radar photographs gives a different relative speed for the track, about 480 knots. It concludes the object was probably a delta-wing fighter

> "delta-wing fighter of a type having a significantly higher climb" performance than the F-8C at 50,000 feet <Cite card="7b2fec96cf493ed7" page={4} q="delta-wing fighter of a type having a significantly higher climb" />

It then raises a Soviet-built MiG-21 operating from Cuba as a possibility it can neither confirm nor rule out: there is no evidence "at hand to suggest or deny any MiG-21 reconnaissance activity" <Cite card="7b2fec96cf493ed7" page={4} q="hand to suggest or deny any MiG-21 reconnaissance activity" />.

## Where the documents disagree

The two sets of papers do not agree with each other on small things, and we leave those as found:

- D022 p3 calls the pilot's aircraft an "F-4C"; the briefing notes and D022 p4 describe an F-8 or F-8C, a Crusader.
- D022 p3 puts the Gyatt's radar as the SPS-43, and p4 says the radar under evaluation was an "AN/SPS-49". We do not resolve which is meant.
- The speed: 3,800 knots in the Navy's account, and about 480 knots relative speed in the CIA analyst's reading of the photographs.

The released title says 1965, which is the date of the memos. The events are in November 1964.

## What we're not claiming

We are not claiming the object was anything exotic, and the CIA's own analyst concluded it was not. What the documents show is narrower: a Navy destroyer and a Navy pilot reported something fast, the radar film was handed to CIA analysts, and the office that flew the A-12 told its own side that it did not think the object was its aircraft. Whether the object was a delta-wing fighter, a Cuban or Soviet reconnaissance flight, or a radar artifact, the documents do not settle. The 3,800-knot figure is the Navy's report as relayed, not something this entry verifies. Every quotation above is verbatim from the released OCR, pinned to its card and page.
