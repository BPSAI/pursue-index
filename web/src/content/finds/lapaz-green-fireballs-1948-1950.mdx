## What the document is

Pages 24–40 of FBI 62-HQ-83894 Section 6 (card `13f86e95aed52840`) are a single transmittal: a 25 May 1950 cover memo from **Lt. Col. Doyle Rees**, Commanding Officer of the 17th District OSI at Kirtland AFB, addressed to Brigadier General Joseph F. Carroll, Director of Special Investigations, USAF. Subject: *Summary of Observations of Aerial Phenomena in the New Mexico Area, December 1948 – May 1950*. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={24} q="Aerial Phenomena" />

Attached to the cover memo are LaPaz's "Seventh Report" of 23 May 1950 (pages 27–31), a frequency-distribution graph (page 32), a one-page analysis of a photograph by Cpl. Lertis E. Stanfield (page 33), and a tabular summary of sightings beginning at page 34.

The 17th District's responsibility for tracking these phenomena had been formalized at a December 1948 liaison meeting that included Fourth Army, AFSWP, the FBI, AEC, the University of California, the Air Force Scientific Advisory Board, AMC, and OSI. The cover memo records that no logical explanation was offered at that meeting; participants concluded only that the phenomena "existed" and should be studied scientifically until satisfactorily explained. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={25} q="phenomena existed" /> The same memo notes the District's concern about "continued occurrence" of such phenomena near sensitive military and government installations.

## LaPaz's eleven differences

The Seventh Report's central section is a numbered enumeration of how the green fireballs depart from typical meteor behavior. The first ten were carried forward from his Second Report of 20 December 1948; the eleventh was added in 1950 from a frequency analysis just completed.

The differences are observational, not interpretive. They are stated as comparisons with the cataloged behavior of meteors:

- **Path geometry.** Most December fireballs traveled in horizontal paths; genuine meteors rarely do. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={27} q="horizontal nature" />
- **Altitude.** Meteor heights are typically of the order of forty or more miles; the December 12 fireball's calculated height was much lower.
- **Velocity.** The December 12 fireball's calculated velocity was lower than typical meteors but considerably greater than V-2 rockets, jet aircraft, or conventional flares.
- **Acoustic signature.** Meteorites penetrating to comparable altitudes are always accompanied by very violent noises; no noises were reported for the December fireballs. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={27} q="violent noises" />
- **Approach direction.** Genuine meteors arrive from all points of the compass with equal frequency; the green fireballs concentrated in approach sectors from the north half of the sky. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={28} q="north half" />
- **Color.** Laboratory test identified the color as consistent with copper-salt emission in a blowpipe flame — rare for genuine meteors.
- **Time-of-day distribution.** A frequency-versus-local-time analysis showed the fireballs' maximum at approximately 2030, coincident with neither the meteor maximum (~0300) nor the meteorite-fall maximum (~1600). <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={29} q="2030" />

The full numbered list (and the contemporaneous correspondence around it) runs across pages 27–31.

## What LaPaz recommended

LaPaz did not propose a single explanation for what the fireballs were. His Seventh Report incorporates a long passage from his own 20 February 1950 letter to Dr. P. H. Wyckoff at the Atmospheric Physics Laboratory, in which — having reviewed all available data — he concludes the fireballs "fall into one of two categories": meteorite falls (the majority), and U.S. guided missiles undergoing tests. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={30} q="meteorite falls" /> The same letter recommends placing Dr. Fred Whipple, Dr. Peter Millman, Dr. L. A. Manning, and Dr. William Crozier in charge of various components of a structured fireball project (radar tracking, dust collection, ground search). LaPaz offers to serve as voluntary consultant. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={31} q="voluntary basis" />

## The sighting tables

Pages 34–40 are a CONFIDENTIAL columnar log of "Sightings of Unknown Aerial Phenomena" reported by the 17th District. Each entry records date, time, number of observers, observer reliability rating (VR Very Reliable, R Reliable, Unk Unknown), location, apparent direction of flight, altitude, color, course (horizontal-to-vertical), apparent speed, and manner of disappearance. A representative entry from December 5, 1948 records two reliable observers near Las Vegas, New Mexico observing a whitish-orange object slightly above 9,000 feet for a few seconds, descending slightly before disappearing. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={35} q="Las Vegas" />

Many entries are categorized in a footnote-keyed evaluation column — `(1)` Green Fireball Phenomena, `(2)` Disk or Variation, `(3)` Probable Meteor. The tables span December 1948 through early 1949 across Las Vegas (NM), Albuquerque, Sandia Base, Los Alamos, Roswell, Alamogordo, Phoenix, El Paso, and Window Rock (AZ), among others.

A separate one-page item, "Sighting No. 175," documents an analysis of a photograph taken at Datil, New Mexico by Cpl. Lertis E. Stanfield of Holloman AFB on 24–25 February 1950. LaPaz's analysis concludes: based on the angular diameter (approximately 1/4 of a degree) and angular velocity (greater than half a degree per minute), the photographed object was not the moon, not Venus or any other planet, and not a bright fixed star slightly out of focus. <Cite card="13f86e95aed52840" page={33} q="Stanfield" />

## What the file establishes

The 25 May 1950 transmittal does not establish that the green fireballs are exotic. It establishes that:

- A credentialed astronomer with a research-mathematics background at the New Mexico Proving Grounds was attached to the 17th District as voluntary consultant for the duration of the investigation.
- He was, by his Seventh Report, comfortable categorizing the phenomena into terrestrial categories (meteorite, missile test) but was not comfortable closing the file without structured radar-and-dust observation by a named scientific team.
- The 17th District itself was treating the recurring observations as a matter for "scientific" study — its phrase — rather than as a public-relations problem or a hoax problem.

LaPaz's report is a working scientist's primary document. It catalogs differences, recommends instrumentation, identifies named collaborators, and quantifies what is missing. The reader can examine the eleven enumerated differences and the eighteen-month sighting table directly and form their own assessment of how secure each entry is.

For the broader institutional context — how the FBI was reading these reports as they arrived from OSI and other agencies — see the [reader's guide to FBI 62-HQ-83894](/finds/fbi-62-hq-83894-readers-guide), Section 6.
