## What the document is

FBI 62-HQ-83894 Section 4 (card `5ecebc7c28a8d8c7`) preserves, on pages 109–113, a small cluster of correspondence about photographs taken in July 1947 by William Presley of Oak Ridge, Tennessee:

- **Page 109** — A 18 February 1949 memorandum from SAC Knoxville to the FBI Director, transmitting a copy of a letter from Colonel C. D. Gasser to G. J. Rathman of the AEC's Security Division. The cover memo summarizes the result: the photographs "are the result of accident or purposeful hoax and are not actually photographs of a 'flying saucer'." <Cite card="5ecebc7c28a8d8c7" page={109} q="accident or purposeful hoax" />
- **Pages 112–113** — The full text of Gasser's 10 February 1949 letter, marked "C O P Y" in vertical marginal letters, explaining the basis of the analysis.

Gasser's title at the time is given as "AMC Engineering Field Officer" at NEPA Project, P. O. Box E, Oak Ridge — meaning he was the Air Materiel Command's man on the ground for engineering matters at the Atomic Energy Commission's Oak Ridge installation.

## The chain of analysis

Gasser's letter establishes the chain of custody and the analytical authority concisely. The photographs were originally received by Major D. J. Grant on site, then forwarded by Gasser's office to the Air Force office of the Chief, Technical Intelligence Division, AMC, Wright-Patterson AFB. The body of the letter quotes Wright-Patterson's analysis verbatim. <Cite card="5ecebc7c28a8d8c7" page={112} q="Technical Intelligence Division" />

The analysis itself is written as three discrete numbered observations of the negatives:

**Observation 1 — Surface relief on the emulsion.** Examined under strong incident light, the emulsion is "raised" at the location of the reported sphere relative to the rest of the negative. The conclusion is that a drop of warm water or developer struck the film at that point and rolled down the surface. <Cite card="5ecebc7c28a8d8c7" page={112} q="raised at that point" /> The diagnostic step is mechanical: tilt the negative under raking light and observe whether the sphere belongs to the silver image or to the surface relief. The letter reports that it belongs to the latter.

**Observation 2 — Continuity across two frames.** When the two negatives are placed side by side with the edges aligned, the so-called "trail" is continuous from one negative onto the next. <Cite card="5ecebc7c28a8d8c7" page={112} q="rolled on from one negative" /> This observation is independent of the first: a real airborne object photographed in a 35mm camera would not produce a single continuous mark crossing the dead space between adjacent frames.

**Observation 3 — Trail in the unexposed margin.** The conclusive finding is given on page 113:

> "Still further proof is shown on the fogged edge of the negative containing only the trail. This is proven by the fact that the trail extends beyond the normal format of the negative. In other words, there is a trail of that section of the negative which was not exposed in the camera." <Cite card="5ecebc7c28a8d8c7" page={113} q="not exposed in the camera" />

The "trail" extends into a region of the negative that was never exposed in the camera at all — physically outside the lens's image circle. Whatever produced the trail interacted with the emulsion *during processing*, not during exposure.

A fourth, briefer observation closes the analysis by pointing to the number of unevenly developed sections of the negative.

## The recommendation

Gasser's letter does not stop at attribution. It recommends declassification of the entire matter and that Mr. Presley be advised of the nature of the images if and when the negatives are returned to him. <Cite card="5ecebc7c28a8d8c7" page={113} q="declassified" /> The intended outcome is that the original photographer be told what the analysis found.

The FBI's cover memo on page 109 records, separately, that Colonel Gasser personally advised that while the photographs are not real, his statements concerning the existence of flying saucers and the opinions previously expressed by him "nevertheless stand as previously stated." <Cite card="5ecebc7c28a8d8c7" page={109} q="nevertheless stand" />

That sentence is unusual to find preserved in an FBI summary memo. It marks an explicit separation between the analysis of one specific piece of physical evidence and the analyst's broader views on the underlying topic. Gasser is on the record as the colonel who concluded these particular negatives were artifacts; he is also on the record as someone whose other professional views about the existence of flying saucers were not retracted by that finding.

## What the document establishes about institutional method

The Presley case produced what is, by 1949 standards, a textbook small forensic examination:

1. Physical chain of custody from observer to AEC to AMC field office to Wright-Patterson.
2. Three independent observations of the negatives, each diagnostic on its own.
3. A specific causal mechanism (a drop of warm water or developer rolling down the emulsion during processing) that accounts for all three observations together.
4. A recommendation to declassify and to notify the original photographer.

What the document does *not* do is treat Presley as a hoaxer. The cover-memo phrase "accident or purposeful hoax" preserves both possibilities; the technical analysis does not require Presley's intent in the production of the artifact, only the developer drop's contact with the film. The negatives could be the result of a careless film-processing step that left a marker at the moment a curious citizen submitted them up the chain.

The value of the entry is that the corpus contains, in its own pages, a complete forensic refutation of one of its July 1947 photographs — written in 1949, at a level of methodological care equivalent to what a contemporary archival analysis might produce. The same release that contains [LaPaz's catalog of differences from meteor behavior](/finds/lapaz-green-fireballs-1948-1950) and the [January 1951 radar-lock teaching case](/finds/oak-ridge-radar-january-1951) also contains this letter. The archive's institutional record on photographic evidence is not credulous.
