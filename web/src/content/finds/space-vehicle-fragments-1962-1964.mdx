## What the documents are

- **USG-UAP-D002** (`21c9fa9b9a543068`): *Interagency Correspondence Concerning Space Fragments, 1962–1964*, 72 pages. The withdrawal sheets on pages 1 and 2 identify it as a folder from the National Security File, Files of Charles E. Johnson, titled "Space Vehicle Fragments - Responsibilities and Procedures" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={1} q="National Security File, Files of Charles E. Johnson" />. Johnson was the NSC staff member who received the Space Council's papers. The folder is filed roughly newest first. It holds memos, meeting attendance lists, a telecon note, NASA press guidance, and many copies of the same two drafts, a public statement and an internal set of guidelines. Several of those drafts appear two or three times. This entry cites the clearest copy.
- **EOP-UAP-D002** (`38e700c6f51082f0`): *Memorandum on Interagency Coordination for the Recovery of Foreign Materiel, May 1963*, 1 page. It is a May 29, 1963 memo from R. W. Hale of the Space Council staff to Dr. Welsh, subject "Conference with Frutkin -- Fragments".

Some wording in the release metadata is the releaser's, not the documents'. The EOP card's title says *Foreign Materiel*. The memo says fragments and pieces. The manifest description for that card says the Department of State and the *Department of War* wanted to use NASA. The memo names "DOD and AF", meaning the Defense Department and the Air Force. It says State wanted to be able to point to NASA, and that DOD and AF wanted NASA as cover. These are separate points in the memo.

The file is about debris from American and Soviet rockets and satellites. A search of the text of both cards finds no use of the words UFO, UAP, unidentified or saucer. The only foreign program named is the Soviet one, once, in a December 1963 note.

## The problem in 1962

On October 2, 1962, Edward C. Welsh, Executive Secretary of the National Aeronautics and Space Council, sent the same memo to NASA Administrator James E. Webb and to McGeorge Bundy at the White House. It had been suggested, he wrote, that "there is need for a decision as to what government agencies should have what responsibilities" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={67} q="there is need for a decision as to what government agencies should have what responsibilities" /> for fragments that fall on U.S. territory. He added: "There are legal as well as operational problems involved" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={67} q="There are legal as well as operational problems involved" />.

Meetings followed on October 17 and November 6, 1962. The attendance list for November 6 includes the AEC, Defense, Justice, NASA, the NSC, the Office of Science and Technology, State, and three Council staff, among them R. W. Hale <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={45} q="Interagency Meeting on Space Vehicle Fragments Tuesday, November 6, 1962" />. The Council staff wrote two papers. One was for public release, the other an internal paper <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={57} q="One is intended for con- sideration as a public release, and the other as a paper of intra-govern- mental understanding." />.

The public statement draft was short. NASA would receive, analyze and identify fragments, and:

> "Fragments identified as coming from vehicles launched by foreign countries will be retained by NASA pending final arrangements for their return to the launching country or for other appropriate disposition." <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={49} q="Fragments identified as coming from vehicles launched by foreign countries will be retained by NASA pending final arrangements for their return to the launching country or for other appropriate disposition." />

The October 18 version had ended at "return to the launching country" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={58} q="return to the launching country." />. The words "or for other appropriate disposition" were Johnson's suggestion. In his October 23 note to Welsh he wrote that he could not see "why the State Department should choke on having it in the public statement", and gave his reason:

> "It stands to reason that if we believe the satellite were hostile that we would not return it to its point of origin." <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={53} q="It stands to reason that if we believe the satellite were hostile that we would not return it to its point of origin." />

Johnson also recast the guidelines as a draft National Security Action Memorandum. Its paragraph on objects collected by Defense teams after a deorbit reads: "Any such objects obtained are to be returned initially to NASA. Subsequent handling will be in accordance with existing intelligence collection procedures." <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={55} q="Any such objects obtained are to be returned initially to NASA. Subsequent handling will be in accordance with existing intelligence collection procedures." /> The NSAM number is blank, and the file has no signed version.

On November 6, Welsh sent Johnson the papers "as revised and agreed to at today's meeting" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={35} q="Herewith are the draft papers as revised and agreed to at today's meeting." />. The November 6 guidelines gave NASA custody throughout. On foreign fragments, NASA "will give the DOD and other responsible agencies access" for intelligence analysis, after which the fragments "will remain in the custody of NASA" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={37} q="NASA will give the DOD and other responsible agencies access, as expeditiously as possible" />. Other paragraphs bring in the AEC for radioactive material, the Smithsonian Institution for collection and analysis, and State for "policy guidance" on disposition.

Then the CIA came in. On December 7, 1962, Deputy Director Marshall S. Carter told Welsh that "the interagency handling of space vehicle fragments is of interest and concern to the United States intelligence community" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={34} q="Review of these documents has clearly indicated that the interagency handling of space vehicle fragments is of interest and concern to the United States intelligence community." />. He placed the papers before the U.S. Intelligence Board for its December 12 meeting. Three days later Johnson told his NSC colleague Thomas Parrott about "the small hassel" [sic] "between DOD and CIA" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={32} q="the small hassel between DOD and CIA concerning the procedural guidelines paper" />. Departmental clearances had been secured, but "later on the Agency clearance somehow got unstuck." He called it a "tempest in a tea cup" that "would have been avoided entirely if the Space Council would have brought the Agency into the ad hoc committee from the beginning" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={32} q="that would have been avoided entirely if the Space Council would have brought the Agency into the ad hoc committee from the beginning." />.

## The DOD draft: intelligence first

On March 1, 1963, Defense returned a revision of the guidelines. The marked-up copy is headed "Revised by DOD 3/1/63" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={18} q="Draft of Nov. 6, 1962 Revised by DOD 3/1/63" />. It strikes "analyzing" from NASA's role. It adds an exception: Defense "is solely responsible for receiving and identifying fragments from vehicles launched by the DoD" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={27} q="is solely responsible for receiving and identifying fragments from vehicles launched by the DoD." />. The largest change is paragraph 3, on foreign fragments. Access becomes delivery:

> "To insure prompt analysis for intelligence purposes, NASA and other U.S. Government agencies will deliver to the DoD as expe- ditiously as possible and on a priority basis fragments (including whole vehicles)" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={27} q="To insure prompt analysis for intelligence purposes, NASA and other U.S. Government agencies will deliver to the DoD as expe- ditiously as possible and on a priority basis fragments (including whole vehicles)" />

Foreign fragments that reach Defense first "will be retained by DoD for analysis" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={27} q="Such foreign fragments which come initially into possession of the DoD will be retained by DoD for analysis." />, and pass to NASA only afterward. Public information on fragments from Defense launches becomes Defense's responsibility. The public statement was edited only to match. A note on that draft reads "Delete bracketed words to conform to DOD revisions of paper dated March 1, 1963." <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={21} q="Delete bracketed words to conform to DOD revisions of paper dated March 1, 1963." />

## NASA's objections

NASA's reply came in two forms. One is an undated letter to Welsh from Arnold W. Frutkin, Director of NASA's Office of International Programs. He asks for the "other US government agencies" wording and the Defense retention sentence to be removed, "since they are inconsistent with the statement in paragraph one" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={25} q="should be deleted since they are inconsistent with the statement in paragraph one that" /> naming NASA as the receiving agency.

The other is Hale's May 29, 1963 memo, the EOP card. Hale met Frutkin on May 28 and had to review the earlier meetings with him "because he was away at the time and now is very negative on the project" <Cite card="38e700c6f51082f0" page={1} q="because he was away at the time and now is very negative on the project." />. Hale lists seven points. Frutkin thought no public announcement was now needed, and that "An Executive Order or statement from the White House would scare people needlessly" <Cite card="38e700c6f51082f0" page={1} q="An Executive Order or statement from the White House would scare people needlessly" />. He thought the problem was overseas, and that State wanted to point to NASA as the responsible agency. Point 4 reads, in part, that DOD and AF want to use NASA for a "cover":

> "but have no intention of letting NASA do anything but return some pieces" <Cite card="38e700c6f51082f0" page={1} q="but have no intention of letting NASA do anything but return some pieces" />

Frutkin also thought "CIA and NASA get along fine" <Cite card="38e700c6f51082f0" page={1} q="CIA and NASA get along fine and can do so in the future on this matter" />, and that NASA should wait for the Intelligence Board. NASA had been drafting legislation, but it would oppose sending anything to Congress "because of the harmful debate it would touch off" <Cite card="38e700c6f51082f0" page={1} q="would oppose sending anything up to Congress in the foreseeable future because of the harmful debate it would touch off." />. The "(legal?)" in that sentence is Hale's own query.

Two days later Welsh asked John A. McCone, as Director of Central Intelligence and chairman of the Board, to act on the Defense draft. He wrote that it had Defense clearance, "but we now understand it has not been coordinated within the intelligence community" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={26} q="but we now understand it has not been coordinated within the intelligence community." />. "Considerable time has now elapsed," he added.

## The public statement and the press line

By October 1963 Welsh was writing to Rusk, McCone, Gilpatric, Wiesner, Webb and Bundy. Agreement "was initially obtained. Subsequently, some variations in preferred language developed" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={23} q="Draft language was circulated and agreement was initially obtained. Subsequently, some variations in preferred language developed." />. Webb had told Chairman Miller of the House Science and Astronautics Committee that legislation was not needed because the Executive Branch was considering the question. Welsh called new meetings for November 6 and November 21, 1963. The attendance lists now include the CIA and Defense Research and Engineering. The public statement was never issued, as far as this file shows.

What the file does show is NASA's press handling of real reentries. A December 16, 1963 note on the SA-5 Saturn launch records two precautions: "13,000 lbs. of sand were substituted for the ballast originally programmed (lead and iron); (2) the Jupiter nose cone was roughed up so that it would burn up during reentry." <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={7} q="13,000 lbs. of sand were substituted for the ballast originally programmed (lead and iron); (2) the Jupiter nose cone was roughed up so that it would burn up during reentry." /> It also notes that "(Most fragments collected to date have been stainless steel pieces of Atlas skin)." <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={8} q="(Most fragments collected to date have been stainless steel pieces of Atlas skin)." /> Without the sand, the vehicle would be about the size of "Soviet vehicles which have already decayed" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={8} q="it is estimated that the vehicle will be of the approximate weight and size of Soviet vehicles which have already decayed" />.

An April 8, 1964 Secret paper gives prepared answers for senior NASA officials on SA-5 and the GT-1 Gemini flight, with casualty odds of one in 50,000 and one in 27,500 launches. "It is suggested that the minimum information be used to satisfy a given query." <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={12} q="It is suggested that the minimum information be used to satisfy a given query." /> A telex from Julian Scheer, NASA's Assistant Administrator for Public Affairs, filed behind a routing slip dated May 26, 1964, sets the line for the SA-6 booster:

> "NO INFORMATION IS BEING VOLUNTEERED." <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={4} q="NO INFORMATION IS BEING VOLUNTEERED." />

> "UNDER NO CIRCUMSTANCES DOES THIS AGENCY (GOVERNMENT) CONJECTURE ON THE ORIGIN OF AN OBJECT." <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={5} q="UNDER NO CIRCUMSTANCES DOES THIS AGENCY (GOVERNMENT) CONJECTURE ON THE ORIGIN OF AN OBJECT." />

The next sentence gives the positive version. The agency "DESIRES TO OBTAIN, ANALYZE, AND ATTEMPT TO ESTABLISH THE ORIGIN OF SUCH AN OBJECT" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={5} q="IT DESIRES TO OBTAIN, ANALYZE, AND ATTEMPT TO ESTABLISH THE ORIGIN OF SUCH AN OBJECT." />, with an announcement after analysis.

## What the file does not show

It does not describe any specific recovery. No fragment is named, located or analyzed in these pages. The Atlas line is the only reference to fragments actually collected. It does not show a final policy. An undated handwritten note asks: "Did USIB take any final action at or since their December meeting?" <Cite card="21c9fa9b9a543068" page={22} q="Did USIB take any final action at or since their December meeting?" /> No answer is filed. The November 15, 1963 draft that Welsh sent for "joint consideration" is not in the released pages. The withdrawal sheet lists the November 1963 drafts without the "open" notation it gives other items. The file contains no signed NSAM and no issued public statement. The releaser's description reaches the same conclusion, that the negotiations did not produce a settled policy. That is the releaser's summary, and the file is consistent with it.

## Where this sits in the archive

The file is in the UAP series because the releasing office put it there. The documents never connect it to UAP. Its value for this archive is as context. It shows the arrangements one part of the government drafted in 1962–64 for objects that fall from the sky: a receiving agency, priority delivery for intelligence analysis, a short public statement, and a press line that volunteers nothing and does not guess at origin. Later UFO-retrieval stories describe a similar pattern. This file does not show that any such pattern was ever applied to anything but rocket and satellite debris.

- [The Hunter-to-Packard Memo, July 18, 1963](/finds/nasc-state-extraterrestrial-policy-memo-1963) comes from the same office, the Space Council staff, in the same year. Robert Packard of State, its addressee, is on the fragments distribution lists and on the attendance lists for the November 1962 and November 1963 meetings. The people overlap. The subject does not. Hunter does not appear in this file, and the fragments papers say nothing about extraterrestrials.
- [What's Not Actually UAP in the PURSUE Archive](/finds/whats-not-uap) covers a 1996 range-safety report on booster failures. That entry treats space hardware as a known explanation for some sightings. This file is the policy side of the same hardware.

## What we're not claiming

We are not claiming this file is about UFOs, or that "foreign fragments" means anything other than Soviet or other foreign space hardware. Johnson's "hostile" satellite is a hypothetical about returning debris to the launching country. Hale's "cover" is his summary of Frutkin's view of Defense intentions. It is not evidence that a cover arrangement operated. The Defense draft's intelligence-first routing is a proposal that, on this file, was never settled. The press guidance concerns announced NASA launches. We are not saying the pattern in this file proves anything about later recovery claims. We are saying it existed, on paper, for space debris. Every quotation above is verbatim from the released text, pinned to its card and page. The selection and framing are ours.
