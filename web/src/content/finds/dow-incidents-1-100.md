## What this card is

Card `b60b84a66715204a` is the Department of War's **Box 7, Incident Summaries 1–100** — 209 declassified pages comprising the first hundred numbered Air Force / Air Defense Command "Unidentified Flying Object" incident reports, almost all dated between June 1947 and early 1948.

Almost every entry uses an identical printed form: **CHECK-LIST – UNIDENTIFIED FLYING OBJECTS**, 26 numbered fields. The fields are:

1. Date
2. Time
3. Location
4. Name of observer
5. Occupation of observer
6. Address of observer
7. Place of observation
8. Number of objects
9. Distance of object from observer
10. Time in sight
11. Altitude
12. Speed
13. Direction of flight
14. Tactics
15. Sound
16. Size
17. Color
18. Shape
19. Odor detected
20. Apparent construction
21. Exhaust trails
22. Weather conditions
23. Effect on clouds
24. Sketches or photographs
25. Manner of disappearance
26. Remarks

The form's existence is itself the story. Within months of the first publicly-reported "flying disc" sighting, the Air Force had standardized intake to the point that a station mechanic in Newfoundland filling out his statement and a test pilot at Muroc filling out his were giving answers to the same 26 fields in the same order. What a witness *didn't* say is preserved as `N/S` (Not Stated) — many fields on many forms come back N/S, which itself is data.

We pick six incidents below to characterize the file's range. Reports we cite are quoted verbatim including the form's idiosyncratic spacing.

## Incident #1: Muroc, July 8, 1947

Incident #1 in this filing system is the same Muroc Army Air Field event preserved in the [FBI's 62-HQ-83894 Section 3](/card/99c12a9c49a91750) as five sworn affidavits — and treated separately on this site as [The Muroc AAF Sightings, July 8, 1947](/finds/muroc-1947). Here, in CHECK-LIST form, the same incident reads as a tabular intake:

> "1. Date | 8 July 1947 | Incident # 1
> 2. Time | 0930
> […]
> enroute to his office and before entering heard one of the local aircraft in the traffic pattern. Looking up he observed the aircraft and slightly to the left he saw 2 silver objects of either spherical or disc-like shape moving about 300 MPH at approximately 8000 ft heading about 320° due north. He immediately called S/Sgt Gerald E. Nauman, T/Sgt Joseph Ruvolo and Miss Jannette Marie Scotte. Witnesses all verified the sighting and the direction of the objects - all three stated that the objects were moving toward Mojave, Calif."[^muroc-checklist]

The CHECK-LIST narrative follows the witness through their attempts to verify what they had seen:

> "Witness was sure it was not an optical illusion or that the objects were not weather balloons since they traveled against prevailing wind and since the speed at which they traveled and the horizontal direction taken indicated that they were not weather balloons. Furthermore they could not remain at the same altitude so consistently if they were weather balloons. Witness then attempted to get personnel (medical officers) to further varify the sighting…"[^muroc-checklist]

Compare this with the same witness's sworn affidavit version in the FBI file, which is first-person legal testimony rather than third-person form-fill ("Looking up, as I always do I observed the aircraft, and looked slightly to the left, whereupon I observed two (2) silver objects…"). Same incident, two filing systems, two literary registers.

## Incident #4: Captain John Paul Stapp, Muroc, July 8, 1947

Two and a half hours after Lt. McHenry's 09:30 sighting at the Billeting Office, Captain John Paul Stapp — at the time a flight test officer at Muroc — filed a separate incident report from a different location on the same base:

> "1. Date | 8 July 1947 | Incident # 4
> 2. Time | 11:50
> 3. Location | Area # 3, Rogers Dry Lake, California.
> 4. Name of observer | Captain John Paul Strapp
> 5. Occupation of observer | Flight Test
> 7. Place of observation | Observation Truck, Area #3, Rodgers Dry Lake, Calif.
> 8. Number of objects | One (1)
> 9. Distance of object from observer | 20,000 feet
> 10. Time in sight | 90 seconds
> 12. Speed | slower than the maximum velocity P-80 aircraft
> […]
> 14. Tactics | rotation or oscillation of slow type (suggested by 2 fins or nobs on the upper surface which crossed each other at intervals
> 18. Shape | resembled a parachute canopy at first then assumed ovular shape
> 20. Apparent construction | It presented a distinct ovular outline, with two (2) projections on the upper surface which might have been thick fins or nobs. These crossed each other at intervals, suggesting either rotation or oscillation for slow type."[^stapp]

(The CHECK-LIST form spells the observer's surname "Strapp" once and "Stapp" elsewhere. Captain Stapp later became famous as the rocket-sled-test pioneer who in 1954 voluntarily underwent 46 g of deceleration at Holloman AFB; in July 1947 he was already at Muroc as a test officer.)

## Incident #17: Kenneth Arnold, near Mt. Rainier, June 24, 1947

Two weeks before Muroc — and twelve days before the press release at Roswell that retracted within hours — civilian pilot **Kenneth Arnold** flew past Mt. Rainier, Washington, and reported nine objects in formation. This is the sighting that gave the English language the term "flying saucer."

The CHECK-LIST form for Incident #17 is among the most spare in the box:

> "1. Date | 24th June 1947
> 2. Time | 1500
> 3. Location | Mt. Ranier, Washington
> 4. Name of observer | Kenneth Arnold
> 5. Occupation of observer | Dealer in fire control supplies holds private pilot's license
> 6. Address of observer | Box 387, Boise, Idaho
> 8. Number of objects | 9
> 9. Distance of object from observer | 20 to 25 miles
> 10. Time in sight | 2-1/2 to 3 minutes
> 11. Altitude | 9,500 ft
> 12. Speed | Approx 150 MPH
> 16. Size | Approximately that of DC-4 - 45 to 50 ft
> 17. Color | mirror like
> 18. Shape | Approximately circular"[^arnold]

The narrative text on the following page reads:

> "Pilot Kenneth Arnold was flying his plane at an altitude of approximately 9,200 feet. He trimmed out plane in direction of Yakima, Washington which was almost directly east of his position and sat in his plane observing the sky and the terrain. […] A bright flash suddenly reflected on the plane. Upon looking to the left and to the north of Mt. Rainier he observed a chain of 9 peculiar looking craft flying from north to south at approximately 9,500 ft elevation and going seemingly in a definite direction of about 170°. Thought at first they were jet aircraft but noticed that every few seconds 2 or 3 of them would dip or change their course slightly just enough to cause the sun to strike them at an angle which reflected brightly on his plane. As they approached Mt. Rainier he could observe their outlines against the snow quite plainly, but couldn't find any tails. Clocked speed and found it to be approximately 150 MPH."[^arnold-narrative]

The CHECK-LIST file reports a clocked speed of approximately 150 MPH — a value sometimes contested in popular accounts of the Arnold sighting that quote much higher figures. The Air Force's filed-of-record number is 150.

## Incident #26: Robert W. Leidy, Newfoundland, July 10, 1947

The corpus is not all military pilots and base personnel. Two days after Muroc, a Pan American Airways station mechanic named Robert W. Leidy — APO 864, Newfoundland — filed a much briefer report:

> "1. Date | 10 July 1947 | Incident # 26
> 2. Time | Between 3:00 and 5:00 PM in the afternoon
> 3. Location | Pan American Airways, Harmon Field, Newfoundland
> 5. Occupation of observer | Station Mechanic
> 7. Place of observation | Traveling in a car returning from fishing.
> 12. Speed | Terrific rate of speed in a circle because of the arcs in the path.
> 14. Tactics | Definite trail and caused clouds to break open as it went through. It looked to be traveling in a circle and left sharp edges to cloud
> 17. Color | No difference in color looked as if object broke the cloud and left this opening.
> 21. Exhaust trails | bluish streak"[^leidy]

Most fields on the Leidy form return `Not Stated`. He was driving back from fishing; he saw something move through clouds; he reported what he could. The form preserves both the data points he could give and the gaps where he could not.

## Incident #30: Captain Charles E. McGee, Lockbourne AAB, January 7, 1948

Six months later, the form has not changed but the witnesses are increasingly uniformed. Captain Charles E. McGee, Assistant Operations Officer at Lockbourne Army Air Base in Columbus, Ohio, filed Incident #30 in the file the same day Captain Thomas Mantell crashed and died near Fort Knox, Kentucky, while pursuing an object in his P-51:

> "1. Date | 7 January 1948 | Incident # 30
> 2. Time | 1925 EST
> 3. Location | Lockbourne Army Air Base, Columbus, Ohio
> 4. Name of observer | Charles E. McGee, Capt. USAF
> 5. Occupation of observer | Asst. Operations Officer (Pilot)
> 7. Place of observation | Runway 23 Overhead approach
> 9. Distance of object from observer | first 4-5 miles Southwest of Base, second time 6-7 miles West and moved westerly in a hovering manner
> 14. Tactics | Flashed on and off, on westward flight appeared to be fading and descending
> 16. Size | Large size of a flood light
> 17. Color | white later yellowish to orange
> 18. Shape | Oval as if looking at spot light
> 25. Manner of disappearance | descending and burning out"[^mcgee]

A separate CHECK-LIST in the same section — Incident **#30c**, also January 7, 1948 — captures DF Operator Mr. Eisele at the Lockbourne control tower seeing what is plausibly the same object 15 minutes earlier:

> "Direction of flight | Appeared to hover but performed eliptical, counter-clock wise course when it descended to horizon (4″) remained stationary (3″) returning original position (3″) […]
> 16. Size | About size of runway light when viewed from 500 ft - apparently enormous - see 9 above
> 17. Color | Red to amber-yellow
> 18. Shape | Circular"[^eisele]

Following pages in the same section preserve PFC Stanley Oliver's statement from the Godman Field control tower at Fort Knox about a separate 1330 CST sighting on the same day, with the surviving narrative noting Col. Hix's office's report of an unidentified object "supposedly some 250 ft to 300 ft in diameter" sighted over Mansville, Ky. — material that became the **Mantell incident** when Captain Thomas Mantell was scrambled to intercept and crashed pursuing it.[^mantell-godman] The CHECK-LIST file preserves the separate intake forms; it does not synthesize them into a single narrative.

## Incident #40: William Albert Rhodes, Phoenix, July 7, 1947

Incident #40 is the William Albert Rhodes sighting at Phoenix on July 7, 1947 — one of the few early incidents accompanied by photographic evidence:

> "1. Date | 7 July 1947 | Incident # 40
> 2. Time | 1600
> 3. Location | Phoenix, Arizona
> 6. Address of observer | 4333 N. 14th St., Phoenix, Arizona
> 12. Speed | 400-600 MPH
> 14. Tactics | Spiraled down from 5000′ to 2000 ft and then ascended at 45° angle
> 15. Sound | Noise like jet on approach - no sound heard while in view
> 16. Size | 20-30 ft
> 17. Color | grey
> 18. Shape | elliptical
> 24. Sketches or photographs | Photos (2)"[^rhodes]

The narrative on the page that follows is unusually detailed and specifies a hypothesis Rhodes himself considered:

> "Object appeared elliptical in shape and had a diameter of 20-30 feet. When first seen it was at about 5,000 ft and traveling from 400 to 600 MPH. Its grey color rendered it difficult to perceive at a distance as it blended with the clouds. […] The object had what appeared to be a cockpit canopy in the center which extended toward the back and beneath the object. […] He also stated that he thought the object was the Navy's new 'Flapjack Plane' but altered his opinion since the object seemed to have no landing gears…"[^rhodes-narrative]

The "Flapjack Plane" reference is to the Chance Vought V-173 / XF5U experimental aircraft — a documented contemporary candidate that the witness himself raised and rejected on the form.

## A multi-witness mass observation (around Inc #69)

The file's later pages include a much different shape of incident: a **multi-witness, multi-pass mass observation** at Twin Falls, Idaho, in which three witnesses progressively counted formations of objects passing overhead in groups of three, five, seven, and finally a flight of "35 to 50":

> "Mrs Hedstrom saw the first object traveling at a terrific rate of speed in a northeasterly direction. It was out of sight before Mr. Hedstrom & their neighbor Mrs. Shultz could see anything. While discussing this sighting all 3 saw 10 similar objects proceed rapidly in the same direction in the form of a triangle. As they were disappearing 3 of the objects on the left flank peeled off and proceeded in a more northerly direction. The remaining objects appeared to close ranks and proceeded on a NE course. Five minutes later they saw another group of 3 objects proceeding in the same direction in triangular formation. Three to 5 minutes another triangular formation of five or six objects were observed going in the same direction. Then a few minutes later the three witnesses saw large group of objects (some 35 to 50 of them) flying in a triangular formation in the same direction. Approximately 20 or 25 minutes after this group of objects were observed similar objects were noted coming back over the city in a Southwesterly direction. Generally they were in groups of 3, 5 and 7 and followed each other at approximately 5-minute intervals. Mrs. Hedstrom then called Detective Richard A. Frazier of the Twin Falls Police Dept., a next door neighbor…"[^twin-falls]

The narrative ends mid-sentence on the bottom of the page; the next page in the corpus is heavily faded and the continuation is partially illegible. (See [Lowry 1949 — what bad OCR looks like](/finds/lowry-1949-ocr-limits) for more on what survives transcription and what doesn't.)

## What the file does and doesn't establish

These are intake forms, not adjudications. The CHECK-LIST does not say *what the object was*; it says *what the witness reported*. Many cases close with the form filed, no follow-up narrative, no investigator's conclusion. The Stapp form notes "rotation or oscillation of slow type"; it does not assert the object actually rotated. The Arnold form records "Approximately circular"; it does not endorse the literal saucer shape that became the era's lasting figure of speech.

What the box *does* establish, when read as a whole:

- A standardized military intake form for "unidentified flying object" reports existed in the U.S. Air Force by mid-1947.
- Test pilots, station mechanics, control tower operators, civilian pilots, civilian housewives, and police officers were all filed under the same form, with the same 26 fields.
- Many incidents had multiple corroborating witnesses; many had only one.
- A non-trivial fraction of fields on most reports come back `N/S` (Not Stated). The reports preserve that absence.
- Cross-references between incidents (e.g., "See Legge account (Incident 60) and Albert Samms' account (Incident 60b)"[^cross-ref]) show the file was consciously linked, not just sequentially numbered.
