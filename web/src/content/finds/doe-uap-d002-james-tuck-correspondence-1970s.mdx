## Why this card matters before its contents

DOE-UAP-D002 is the first Department of Energy document to enter this index, and the first card in which a Manhattan Project alumnus's *personal* — not institutional — UAP correspondence is the primary material. The find is not a disclosure of classified knowledge. It is the documentary fact that **James L. Tuck pursued UAP as a private intellectual interest in the 1970s**, and corresponded with at least one Los Alamos security-force veteran and one ball-lightning correspondent about it.

Tuck was a British-born physicist who joined the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos under Oppenheimer, and after the war led Project Sherwood — the Los Alamos plasma physics effort that founded U.S. controlled-fusion research. He was one of the most credentialed alumni of the wartime weapons program. His extant personal letters about UAP are therefore a small but historically substantive primary source for how scientists with that level of access read the phenomenon.

The card is four pages. Two are a single inbound letter to Tuck recalling Los Alamos sightings from 1948–1951. One is a 16 December 1970 letter Tuck wrote out to the U.S. Army Engineering School. One is a later inbound letter on ball lightning.

## The 1948–1951 Los Alamos sightings — a security officer's recollection

Pages 1–2 of the card are a two-page response addressed *to* Tuck, signed by an individual whose name is redacted under (b)(6). The correspondent identifies themselves as a member of what they call the "Protection Force" — the Los Alamos site security force — during the period in question. The letter regrets they cannot answer Tuck's attached form on specific times and dates, then offers from memory:

> "I can however, tell you that during the years 1948 through 1951 several sightings of green lights were made at Los Alamos. These usually occurred during the early part of the night, nine to eleven, and were usually near the Jemez Mountains." <Cite card="8156067f7ff4b983" page={1} q="1948 through 1951" />

The correspondent describes "green light weaving in and out of mountain peaks," notes that all sightings were "reported to the Protection Force Headquarters and should be a matter of record on their logs," and recalls one specific daylight event:

> "I also recall one instance of five objects flying over Los Alamos in the afternoon. They were flying from southeast to northwest and appeared to be flying in formation." <Cite card="8156067f7ff4b983" page={1} q="five objects" />

They identify themselves (anonymously, via redaction) as one of "the five or six people" who saw those five objects, and name a second redacted Protection Force member Tuck might also contact. They close by suggesting that if Tuck can obtain access to the Protection Force logs for the period, the time-and-date data will be available there. <Cite card="8156067f7ff4b983" page={2} q="Protection Force logs" />

The 1948–1951 timeframe is the same window Dr. Lincoln LaPaz catalogued in his Seventh Report to the OSI 17th District, separately preserved in FBI 62-HQ-83894 Section 6 and discussed in the [LaPaz green fireballs entry](/finds/lapaz-green-fireballs-1948-1950). The Tuck correspondence does not add new sightings to that record; it documents that decades later, a security-force veteran was being asked about them by a senior Los Alamos physicist, and that the veteran's recollection of green lights near the Jemez at 2100–2300 hours is independently consistent with the LaPaz catalog.

## Tuck's own 1970 letter — the Condon Report and an atmospheric-vortex recipe

Page 3 is a single-page outbound letter on what appears to be Los Alamos letterhead ("P-DO" header), signed "James L. Tuck" and dated **16 December 1970**. It is addressed to a redacted recipient at the U.S. Army Engineering School, Fort Belvoir, Virginia, Department of Mechanical and Technical Equipment.

The body is short and specific:

> "As agreed in our telephone conversation of today's date, I would like to have the recipe that was used for the simulated atomic bomb demonstrations. We are interested in the large atmospheric vortices which are produced as reported in the book 'Scientific Study of Unidentified Flying Objects' by Dr. Edward U. Condon." <Cite card="8156067f7ff4b983" page={3} q="large atmospheric vortices" />

The reference is to the Condon Committee's 1968 *Scientific Study of Unidentified Flying Objects* — the University of Colorado study commissioned by the Air Force that recommended the closure of Project Blue Book. Tuck is asking the Army for the formula used to produce a controlled mushroom-cloud demonstration so he can study the *vortex* it generates. He is not asking about UFOs directly; he is asking about an atmospheric phenomenon discussed in a UFO study report.

This is the most direct documentary evidence in the card of Tuck's personal research interest. He is a senior Los Alamos physicist requisitioning experimental material from a sister agency in order to follow up on a specific physical mechanism cited in the Condon Report. The letter is a working-scientist's request, not an opinion piece.

## The ball-lightning exchange

Page 4 is a one-page inbound letter, undated except for "November 28," addressed "Dear Jim" and signed under a (b)(6) redaction. The text references Tuck's "interesting report on ball Lightning" and encloses comments by James M. McCampbell from McCampbell's *UFOLOGY*, dated to 1976 — "appearing on the new non-fiction shelf at the Mesa Library, call number 629.1338 M125u." <Cite card="8156067f7ff4b983" page={4} q="UFOLOGY" />

The correspondent adds a personal aside on Einstein and the unified field theory. <Cite card="8156067f7ff4b983" page={4} q="unified field theory" /> The 1976 publication date of McCampbell's book places this letter no earlier than that year. The exchange documents that Tuck had himself prepared a report on ball lightning — a recurring scientific candidate for some UFO observations — and was sharing it with at least one correspondent in his personal network.

## What the card does and does not establish

The four pages establish, on the documentary record:

- That James L. Tuck, a Los Alamos physicist of Manhattan Project rank, corresponded in the 1970s with at least one Los Alamos Protection Force veteran and one independent correspondent about UAP and related atmospheric phenomena.
- That he wrote to the U.S. Army Engineering School on 16 December 1970 requesting experimental material specifically to study atmospheric vortices cited in the Condon Report.
- That he authored or contributed a report on ball lightning circulated in his personal correspondence by 1976.
- That a Los Alamos security-force veteran's first-person recollection of 1948–1951 green-light sightings near the Jemez Mountains exists in the document trail — consistent in date range and geography with the LaPaz green-fireball catalog separately documented in FBI 62-HQ-83894 Section 6.

The four pages do **not** establish:

- Anything about what Tuck knew or did not know through his Los Alamos classified access about UAP.
- That his interest was an official program responsibility rather than a personal one. The letterhead on page 3 is Los Alamos institutional, but the framing of the request — and the personal-correspondence framing of pages 1–2 and 4 — fits a working scientist following a published-literature lead, not a tasked investigation.
- The identity of any of the redacted correspondents.
- Any institutional Department of Energy position on UAP. DOE-UAP-D002 is *personal* correspondence preserved in DOE custody, not a DOE policy document.

## Why this card is in this archive

It is in this archive because primary correspondence from a working Manhattan Project–era physicist about unidentified aerial phenomena is rare, brief, and historiographically substantive. The Tuck letters are not a revelation. They are documentation. They show that the question "what were the green lights over Los Alamos in 1948–1951" was being asked twenty years later by one of the people most credentialed to ask it, and that the answer trail led through the Condon Report, Project Sherwood–adjacent vortex physics, and ball-lightning literature rather than through any disclosure of classified holdings.
