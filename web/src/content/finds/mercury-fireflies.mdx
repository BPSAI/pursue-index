## What these documents are

Four cards in the archive hold air-to-ground audio from the final three orbital flights of Project Mercury:

- `6b12c3ddc6a96008` (**NASA-UAP-D013**) — **Scott Carpenter**, *Aurora 7* (MA-7), May 24, 1962
- `35e0a7cff95a3e93` (**NASA-UAP-D012**) — **Wally Schirra**, *Sigma 7* (MA-8), October 3, 1962
- `1bc28d0e03b79e35` (**NASA-UAP-D010**) and `ef779c3f0f7ba4d3` (**NASA-UAP-D011**) — **Gordon Cooper**, *Faith 7* (MA-9), May 15, 1963

They belong to the same story, which begins on a flight not in this set. On February 20, 1962, on *Friendship 7*, **John Glenn** became the first American to orbit the Earth and reported, at his first orbital sunrise, that the capsule was surrounded by a cloud of tiny luminous particles. He called them **"fireflies,"** and for a few hours they were a genuine open question — thousands of glowing specks outside a spacecraft, drifting with it, of unknown origin. These four cards are what the government's UAP release preserves of what happened next: the same phenomenon, seen and reasoned about on the three flights that followed Glenn's.

Two of these tapes (the two Cooper cards) came off the recording as waveform-distinct stereo but carry the same air-to-ground program on both channels; all four were transcribed as single-channel audio. The speaker labels are automated and, on the mission-loop excerpts, mostly separate the astronaut from the capsule communicator ("CAPCOM"). Attribution below rests on the mission each card belongs to — the astronaut named on the flight — not on the automated labels.

## Carpenter: "they look exactly like snowflakes"

The resolution effectively arrives on the very next flight. Scott Carpenter, on *Aurora 7* three months after Glenn, sees the same particles and reaches immediately for the analogy that turned out to be right:

> "I have some more of the white particles in view below the capsule. They appear to be traveling exactly my speed." <Cite card="6b12c3ddc6a96008" page={1} q="white particles in view below the capsule" />

> "Their motion is random. They look exactly like snowflakes to me." <Cite card="6b12c3ddc6a96008" page={1} q="They look exactly like snowflakes to me" />

Houston reads it back to confirm the word, and Carpenter re-confirms it on a second sighting — "They definitely look like snowflakes this time." <Cite card="6b12c3ddc6a96008" page={1} q="They definitely look like snowflakes this time" /> "Snowflakes" is not a throwaway image. Carpenter is the astronaut who, in the popular history of Mercury, is credited with cracking the firefly puzzle — the particles were frost and ice crystals shed from the skin of the capsule, knocked loose and lit by the low sun. The word he keeps choosing on the tape is the explanation.

## Schirra: "seem to come from the capsule itself"

Wally Schirra, on *Sigma 7* five months later, supplies the other half of the answer — where the particles come from:

> "I have some pretty stars in sight and also have the little white objects that seem to come from the capsule itself and drift off." <Cite card="35e0a7cff95a3e93" page={1} q="seem to come from the capsule itself" />

That single clause — *come from the capsule itself* — is the whole resolution stated as an observation rather than a theory. Schirra explicitly connects it to Glenn's original report, borrowing Glenn's own description of the particles' shape:

> "You can actually see the particle there flying off, and as John described it as a lathe shaving. That's a very good description of it." <Cite card="35e0a7cff95a3e93" page={1} q="as John described it as a lathe shaving" />

Schirra's excerpt is not entirely tidy — he also notes, honestly, an unexplained moment: "Getting a real burst of light in the window, and I really don't know what it is." <Cite card="35e0a7cff95a3e93" page={1} q="Getting a real burst of light in the window, and I really don't know what it is" /> It is worth leaving that in. The same astronaut who has just calmly located the "fireflies" as capsule shedding also flags a thing he genuinely cannot account for, and does not pretend otherwise. That is the texture of a real observer at work, not a tidied-up narrative.

## Cooper: "John's fireflies," and the thruster that makes them

Gordon Cooper, on *Faith 7* a year after Glenn, closes the loop twice over. First he names the phenomenon after its discoverer, treating it as an established feature of the flight:

> "I am observing John's fireflies drifting away from me. I can observe them sort of departing from the spacecraft." <Cite card="1bc28d0e03b79e35" page={1} q="I am observing John's fireflies drifting away from me" />

> "Fireflies appear to be white, very whitish, with almost a green like real fireflies." <Cite card="1bc28d0e03b79e35" page={1} q="Fireflies appear to be white, very whitish, with almost a green like real fireflies" />

Then, in the second Cooper excerpt, he does something none of the earlier reports quite managed — he *produces* the particles on command, tying them directly to the spacecraft:

> "Each time I fire a pitch-down thruster, I get a shower of these little fireflies." <Cite card="ef779c3f0f7ba4d3" page={2} q="I get a shower of these little fireflies" />

A shower of "fireflies" on every thruster firing is about as clean a demonstration of spacecraft origin as an astronaut could offer without a laboratory: the particles are not out there in space, they are being shaken off the vehicle. Earlier in the same pass Cooper describes them under changing light as "small luminescent particles drift away from you," <Cite card="ef779c3f0f7ba4d3" page={1} q="These small luminescent particles drift away from you" /> appearing in that light "brilliant white without any green at all in them." <Cite card="ef779c3f0f7ba4d3" page={1} q="brilliant white without any green at all in them" /> (The Cooper excerpts are unambiguously his flight — the CAPCOM, Wally Schirra, addresses him on the loop by name: "Gordon, this is Wally." <Cite card="ef779c3f0f7ba4d3" page={1} q="Gordon, this is Wally" />)

## A second light on the same tape — this one identified

Cooper's *Faith 7* excerpt also contains a different bright object, and it is a useful contrast because Cooper knows exactly what it is. MA-9 carried a **flashing-beacon experiment** — a small sphere ejected from the capsule with a xenon strobe, to test whether an astronaut could acquire a flashing light in orbit. Cooper spends part of the pass hunting for it — "I've been looking for the flashing beacons" <Cite card="ef779c3f0f7ba4d3" page={2} q="I've been looking for the flashing beacons" /> — and then finds it:

> "There's light in sight. It is below me... it is flashing now. It is the light. It's quite bright, quite discernible... appears to be about 10 to 12 miles away." <Cite card="ef779c3f0f7ba4d3" page={2} q="it is flashing now. It is the light" />

On the same tape, then, are two lights: the "fireflies," initially unexplained and resolved by observation into capsule debris, and the flashing beacon, a bright unfamiliar light that is nonetheless fully identified because Cooper deployed it himself. The pairing is the whole editorial point of the Mercury firefly record: a strange light in space is not, by itself, a mystery; it is a question, and these astronauts answered theirs.

## Why surface these

The Mercury fireflies are a **resolved** phenomenon — the standard account, that they were ice and frost particles shed from the capsule and lit by the sun, is exactly what these four astronauts are describing as they watch. That is precisely why the cards are worth surfacing. Included in a government UAP release, they could read as four astronauts reporting an unexplained luminous swarm. Heard in sequence, they are the opposite: a mystery being *closed*, on the record, by the observers themselves — Carpenter's "snowflakes," Schirra's "from the capsule itself," Cooper's thruster-triggered "shower." The archive keeps them for the same reason it keeps the [Apollo 11 debriefing](/finds/apollo-11-debriefing)'s sun-glint "laser" and the [Apollo 17 debriefing](/finds/apollo-17)'s three identified phenomena: they show trained observers handling the unexplained well. They are the Mercury-era companion to the Apollo light-flash cluster — the [Apollo 12](/finds/apollo-12-light-flashes) and [Apollo 14](/finds/apollo-14-light-flashes) crews reasoning their way to "internal, not external," a decade of astronauts reasoning carefully about lights they could not immediately place.

## A note on method and its limits

These are aged 1962–63 mission tapes transcribed by automated speech-to-text; the readings are careful but not canonical, and a few numeric read-backs and cross-talk passages are imperfectly transcribed and are not quoted here. Card assignment to a named astronaut is by mission, which is secure; the automated speaker labels within each tape are not relied upon for attribution.
