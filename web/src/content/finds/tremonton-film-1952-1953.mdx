## What the documents are

Release 6 carries three paper records of one short piece of film, plus the film itself:

- **DOW-UAP-D102** (`6c3f5372eae29724`): *Project Blue Book File on Tremonton Film, Utah, 1952*, 156 pages. Record cards, teletypes, the Air Force and Navy lab analyses, the internal memos about the press release, and later correspondence running to 1956.
- **DOW-UAP-D103** (`49e0690a6f5bb74f`): the Blue Book photo file for the same case, five pages.
- **DOW-UAP-D105** (`fec5b139eb7b0a4f`): *U.S. Air Force Flying Discs File, 1952*. It holds the Headquarters memo that sent the film to the Navy.
- **DOW-UAP-PR159** (`2fde9a0c4dee513c`): *Historical Film of Reported UFOs, Utah, 1952*. This is the footage, released as a video card. It has no text layer, so nothing below quotes it.

The sighting itself is simple. The Blue Book record card lists the source as "CPO, USN", reports 10 to 12 objects, and says the observer "exposed 30 feet of 16 mm color motion picture" film <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={2} q="exposed 30 feet of 16 mm color motion picture" />. The photo file adds that his wife noticed the objects first, and that there were "No reference points by which to estimate speed, distance or size" <Cite card="49e0690a6f5bb74f" page={2} q="No reference points by which to estimate speed, distance or size" />. That second line matters for everything that follows. With nothing in the frame to measure against, no lab could fix how far away the objects were, and without distance there is no size and no speed.

## October 1952: "possibly the best documentary evidence"

The Air Force did not treat the film as a curiosity. On 24 October 1952 the Directorate of Intelligence sent it to the Director of Naval Intelligence with this opening line:

> "The attached movies (see Incl. 1) are possibly the best documentary evidence yet obtained on unidentified flying objects." <Cite card="fec5b139eb7b0a4f" page={26} q="documentary evidence yet obtained on unidentified flying objects." />

The same memo explains why it went to the Navy at all. The film had already "been analyzed by an Air Force photographic laboratory", but "it is our desire to obtain an independent analysis by a separate laboratory" <Cite card="fec5b139eb7b0a4f" page={26} q="it is our desire to obtain an independent analysis by a" />. That is a request for a second opinion, and it got one.

## Two labs, one direction

The Air Force lab's analysis is in D102. It is unsigned in the released copy, but its findings match what the February 1953 memo below attributes to "The Air Force lab analysis". On brightness it says:

> "It is believed to be reasonably certain that the reflection brightness of these objects exceeds that of any natural diffusing type of reflecting object, such as white birds against the sky." <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={100} q="of these objects exceeds that of any natural diffusing type of reflecting object," />

It goes on: "Either a large metallic surface of nearly flat shape, or a self-luminous source, is thought to be responsible" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={100} q="Either a large metallic surface of nearly flat shape, or a self-luminous source," />. Its conclusions describe ten objects, "all alike in observed characteristics of movement and size" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={102} q="all alike in observed characteristics of movement and size." />.

The Navy's Photographic Interpretation Center went further. As the February memo summarizes it, the Navy report says the objects are:

> "Self-luminous or light sources" … "Could not be aircraft or balloons" … "No bird is known is reflect enough light to cause the images" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={39} q="No bird is known is reflect enough light to cause the images" />

("is reflect" [sic], as typed.)

The Navy's own May 1953 progress report is in the file too, and it is more careful than the summary. It says its results "do not necessarily represent the official attitude" of the Navy or the Center <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={71} q="do not necessarily represent the official attitude" />. Its speed figures rest on a distance that its December 1952 letter admits "was arbitrarily set at five miles" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={132} q="This was arbitrarily set at" />. It also records that outside experts in astronomy and physics could not explain the images under the conditions of the filming, "nor were they able to recognize these objects as man-made" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={77} q="nor were they able to recognize these objects as man-made." />.

## 11 February 1953: the plan for the press

This is the page that makes the file worth reading. It is an ATIC Memorandum for Record dated 11 February 1953, and it runs across three pages of D102. The pages are out of order in the file: the memo reads p37, then p39, then p38.

It opens with a promise. Captain Ruppelt had been told that the press knew about the movies, and that the Pentagon's public-information office and others believed they should be released under the promise "that no information on the subject of Unidentified Flying Objects would be withheld" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={37} q="information on the subject of Unidentified Flying Objects would be withheld." />.

The problem was the lab reports. The memo records a worry that if the findings of the Air Force and Navy labs were not released too, the press would think they "contained some "hot" material and that the Air Force was holding back some important data." ATIC agreed that this "could create a great deal of excitement" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={37} q="and that if it did happen it could create a great deal of excitement." />.

The memo then gives the Air Force lab's conclusion, "They are reasonably certain that the brightness of the images on the film exceeds that of any bird" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={37} q="They are reasonably certain that the brightness of the images" />, and the Navy's, quoted above. It records that "two astronomers who heard a Navy briefing" said the Navy's way of measuring brightness "was wrong, therefore, the results of the entire study were wrong" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={39} q="of each spot was wrong, therefore, the results of the entire study were wrong." />. Then comes the decision. The movies would be released with no comment on what they were, and the plan was to:

> "hope that no questions were asked about the Navy report. This is a "calculated" risk of another big "flap"." <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={39} q="hope that no questions were asked about the" />

The memo lists a second option: get more data first. For the gull theory that meant filming gulls in bright sun, and "At this time of the year Florida is the only place where sunshine and gulls can be assured" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={39} q="At this time of the year Florida is the only place" />. Its conclusion, on p38, spells out the fallback line if the movies had to go out quickly. Say that the Air Force "cannot positively identify the objects but they are reasonably sure that they are balloons or gulls" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={38} q="the Air Force cannot positively identify the objects but they are reasonably" />. If anyone asked for the lab reports, "state that due to the meager data they used, the reports must be discounted" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={38} q="state that due to the meager data they used, the reports must be discounted." />. And:

> "It would be rather poor policy to state that the Navy had made an error in the beginning of their analysis." <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={38} q="It would be rather poor policy to state that the Navy had made an error in" />

## The press line, drafted and redrafted

The file keeps the drafts. A teletype from Captain Ruppelt dated 3 February 1953 carries "data for press release on Tremonton movies" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={78} q="regarding data for press release on Tremonton movies." />. Its text, continued on p80, already says that "Movies of sea gulls taken under similar lighting conditions showed the sea gulls appearing as bright spots of light very similar to those in the movies", and ends with the line that "the Air Force contemplates no future action will be taken to identify the objects" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={80} q="Movies of sea gulls taken under similar lighting conditions showed the sea gulls" />.

A Headquarters draft of 26 February opens far more warmly. It says the color film "provides an instance of a sighting being substantiated by motion pictures" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={42} q="This color film provides an instance of a sighting being" />. Its fourth paragraph says the images came from reflective surfaces "[such as seagulls or balloons]" or from bodies with "high intensity light sources" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={42} q="[such as seagulls or balloons]" />.

ATIC's reply of 27 February, drafted under Ruppelt's name, concurs and suggests keeping the gulls and balloons in that sentence. It gives the reason plainly: wording it that way "indicates the Air Force has given this possibility some thought yet it does not state that we can prove they are balloons or sea gulls" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={81} q="this possibility some thought yet it does not state that we can prove they are" />.

## The file argues with itself

Read end to end, D102 does not reach one conclusion:

- The Blue Book record card is marked "Other BIRDS" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={2} q="Other BIRDS" />, and a file divider reads "BIRDS (SEAGULLS)" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={63} q="BIRDS (SEAGULLS)" />.
- The Project 10073 worksheet (ATIC Test Form 334) has its final-evaluation box ticked "Unknown". Its comments list "Birds - snow geese migrating, or seagulls" as one possibility among three <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={58} q="Birds - snow geese migrating, or seagulls" />.
- The photo file gives a "Prob Explanation -- Pillow balloons or Birds" <Cite card="49e0690a6f5bb74f" page={2} q="Prob Explanation" />.
- A 1 June 1956 memo, written for the Air Force press room about a recently released movie, closes the case: "The unanimity of opinion present in all evaluations made in this case leaves little reasonable doubt that the UFOs in the Newhouse film were indeed seagulls" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={127} q="The unanimity of opinion present in all evaluations" />. It rests that on seagulls said to be identifiable "in some of the frames" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={126} q="identifiable seagulls in some of the" />.

In our reading, "unanimity" is hard to square with the rest of the file. The same file holds a Navy finding that no bird could produce the images and an Air Force lab finding that the images were brighter than white birds. It also holds a worksheet ticked Unknown. The 1956 memo does not mention any of these.

## Custody: which film, and where

The film's paper trail has its own gaps. In January 1954 ATIC wrote to Naval Intelligence trying to find "the original of a 35 mm film" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={46} q="locate the original of a 35 mm film of unidentified flying objects" />. The record card, the Navy report and the 1953 Air Force letters all call it 16 mm. The Navy center's March 1954 reply points out the mismatch: "Reference (a) referred to the original film as being 35mm." It also says the center had "never had possession of the original film and does not have information concerning its whereabouts" <Cite card="6c3f5372eae29724" page={45} q="original film and does not have information concerning its whereabouts." />. What the Navy held was a 16 mm copy and 35 mm blow-ups. Any reading of the film has to carry that caveat, including of the PR159 video card: the file does not say which generation of copy any given analysis used.

## What we're not claiming

We are not claiming the Tremonton objects were anything unusual. Gulls and pillow balloons are serious candidates, and the file says why: soaring gulls, a known pillow-balloon program, and no reference points in the frame. We are also not claiming that either lab was right. The Navy's brightness method was challenged at the time, and the file says so. Our claim is narrower. As we read them, the documents show an Air Force office deciding to release the film without comment on what the objects were, in the hope that no questions would be asked about the Navy report, and wording the release to mention gulls and balloons without claiming it could prove them. Our reading is that the file's closing "unanimity" leaves out evidence in the same file. That is our interpretation, marked as such.
