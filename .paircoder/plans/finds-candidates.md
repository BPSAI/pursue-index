## Candidate 1: William Albert Rhodes Photographs — Phoenix, 1947–1950

**Title:** "Rhodes Photographs (1947–1950) — The Long Path to Skepticism"

**Card(s):**
- Card `13f86e95aed52840` ("62-HQ-83894_Section_6")
- war.gov URL: https://www.war.gov/medialink/ufo/release_1/65_hs1-834228961_62-hq-83894_section_6.pdf

**Page reference:** Pages 170–184 (June 1952 FBI memoranda; original photo intake in 1947 narratives referenced earlier in this section)

**Hook:** A photographer in Phoenix delivered physical negatives to Army Air Force Intelligence in 1947; three years later, the FBI is tracking their location and the subject's media strategy, revealing how institutional uncertainty about documentation persists across multiple agencies and years.

**The find:** William Albert Rhodes photographed what he claimed was a "flying disc" over Phoenix on August 30, 1947. Rather than being settled quickly, the photographs became a multi-agency custody problem. FBI memoranda dated June 1952 (five years after capture) show the Bureau tracking: where the negatives currently reside, whether Rhodes granted True Magazine access, whether Air Force Intelligence still holds them. The June 10 and June 17 memos reveal no consensus on classification status, ownership, or distribution rights. The implicit detail: the photographs *are still in circulation internally* and officials want to know where. This is not a debunking; it's documentation of institutional friction around physical evidence.

**Why this not that:** This beats the "famous sighting" angle because the story is institutional—how do agencies manage physical claims?—not phenomenological. It's dry, archival, and verifiable. The reader can see the exact friction points (pages 170–184) where bureaucracy and uncertainty meet.

## Candidate 2: Lincoln LaPaz and the Los Alamos "Green Fireballs" — Scientist's Analysis, Not Debunking

**Title:** "Dr. Lincoln LaPaz and the Los Alamos Fireballs (1948–1950) — Green Light, Unanswered Questions"

**Card(s):**
- Card `13f86e95aed52840` ("62-HQ-83894_Section_6")
- Includes formal report from University of New Mexico, Institute of Meteoritics
- war.gov URL: https://www.war.gov/medialink/ufo/release_1/65_hs1-834228961_62-hq-83894_section_6.pdf

**Page reference:** Pages 24–40 (Lt. Col. Doyle Rees OSI cover memo, May 25, 1950; Lincoln LaPaz's formal analysis, May 23, 1950; detailed sighting tables with dates, locations, observer credentials; photograph of sighting #175 by Cpl. Lertis E. Stanfield, Feb. 24–25, 1950)

**Hook:** A credentialed astronomer who consulted for U.S. Air Force Intelligence listed 11 specific ways "green fireballs" observed near Los Alamos differed from known meteors — and recommended scientific investigation of objects he suspected might be U.S. missiles.

**The find:** Lincoln LaPaz, director of the University of New Mexico's Institute of Meteoritics, compiled 72 confirmed observations of unusual "green fireballs" over Los Alamos between December 1948 and May 1950. His formal May 23, 1950 report to Lt. Col. Doyle Rees (OSI commander) is methodical: 11 catalogued differences from meteor behavior (horizontal vs. vertical paths, no acoustic signature, peculiar green color, sudden appearance at full brightness, absence of debris trails). Rather than dismissing them, LaPaz proposed two categories: meteorite falls and "U.S. guided missiles undergoing tests." He notes the curious lack of supporting infrastructure (no dust clouds, no wreckage) and recommends involving Fred Whipple, Peter Millman, and other credentialed scientists. The cover memo from Rees emphasizes: "continued occurrence of unexplained phenomena of this nature in the vicinity of sensitive military and government installations is cause for concern." This is not a "solved mystery" — it's a scientist saying *I cannot explain this using standard categories, and this bothers me enough to write it down formally*.

**Why this not that:** LaPaz brings institutional authority and scientific method. The sighting table (pages 34–40) offers dates, observer reliability ratings, direction vectors, and color descriptions — raw material readers can inspect. The green-vs.-meteor distinction is observable. This avoids "believers vs. skeptics" framing and stays in the empirical register.

## Candidate 3: Oak Ridge Radar-Visual Tracking, January 1951 — The Interception Attempt

**Title:** "Oak Ridge, January 1951 — Radar Lock, No Visual Confirmation"

**Card(s):**
- Card `f85532f0514320be` ("62-HQ-83894_Section_7")
- Also referenced in Card `13f86e95aed52840` (summary tables)
- war.gov URL: https://www.war.gov/medialink/ufo/release_1/65_hs1-834228961_62-hq-83894_section_7.pdf

**Page reference:** Page 73 (Air Force official analysis of radar phenomena; the Oak Ridge incident detailed in an analytical framework, not as a fresh sighting report)

**Hook:** Two Air Force aircraft obtained a radar lock on an unidentified target over Oak Ridge, made three intercept passes, and each time the radar contact led them first upward, then downward toward a point on the ground — pattern consistent with temperature inversion reflection, not hostile object.

**The find:** An Air Force document labeled "Radar Sightings" (page 73) includes a case study from January 1951 near Oak Ridge, Tennessee. Two Air Force pilots at 7,000 feet altitude attempted to intercept a radar target. The target appeared on their scope at an elevation 10–25 degrees above them. The pilots made three passes attempting to close, but the radar trace led them toward ground — first upward, then down to a specific point. The Air Force analysis suggests this is evidence of temperature inversion reflection (warm air layer bending radar returns from ground objects). The incident is presented not as a mystery but as a teaching example: why some radar contacts that appear anomalous are explained by atmospheric optical phenomena. The document explicitly notes hundreds of "fruitless intercept efforts" driven by radar misidentification. This is methodologically valuable — it shows how the Air Force is reasoning about its own data and its own pilots' reports.

**Why this not that:** This entry documents institutional learning: the Air Force is teaching itself how to interpret radar artifacts. It also acknowledges the interception attempts happened (not hidden, not downplayed). Readers get the primary mechanism (temperature inversion) and can evaluate it against the radar-lock description.

## Candidate 4: Kenneth Arnold's June 24, 1947 Account — Primary Witness Statement in Full

**Title:** "Kenneth Arnold, June 24, 1947 — The Sighting in His Own Words"

**Card(s):**
- Card `4844321219e306af` ("62-HQ-83894_Section_2")
- war.gov URL: https://www.war.gov/medialink/ufo/release_1/65_hs1-834228961_62-hq-83894_section_2.pdf

**Page reference:** Pages 164–166 (Arnold's own written account, filed in FBI record; includes his technical observations, altitude estimates, speed calculations, and explicit rejection of atmospheric explanations)

**Hook:** The most famous UFO pilot's own account, in his own voice, filed with the FBI — no intermediary, no editorial frame. He explicitly tested whether he was seeing reflections or mirage.

**The find:** Kenneth Arnold's written statement (pages 164–166) details his June 24, 1947 sighting over the Cascade Mountains in Washington. He was searching for a downed marine transport at ~9,500 feet when he observed a chain of nine objects traveling north-to-south at ~300 mph. His account is procedural: he timed their passage between two peaks (Mt. Rainier and Mt. Adams) at one minute and forty-two seconds; he estimated a five-mile formation length by parallax; he noted the absence of tails, which "bothered" him. Crucially, he addresses methodological concerns directly: "A number of news men and experts suggested that I might have been seeing reflections or even a mirage. This I know to be absolutely false, as I observed these objects not only through the glass of my airplane but turned my airplane sideways where I could open my window and observe they with a completely unobstructed view." He also compares the objects' brightness to known reference objects (DC-4 aircraft) and adjusts for distance. The account is not defensive but precise — the detail that stands out is that he noted the objects' behavior against snow-covered mountain ridges as fixed visual references for distance and trajectory. No dramatic claims; technical observation.

**Why this not that:** Arnold is the historical anchor of modern UFO reporting, but the *document* value is that his original statement is calm, specific, and responsive to skeptical challenges he anticipated. It shows the evidentiary standard he applied to himself. Readers get his voice unmediated, and can see how an experienced pilot reasoned about visual observation at altitude.

## Candidate 5: William Presley's Oak Ridge Photographs, July 1947 — Hoax, Documentation, Lesson

**Title:** "Oak Ridge Photographs (July 1947) — False Evidence, Real Process"

**Card(s):**
- Card `5ecebc7c28a8d8c7` ("62-HQ-83894_Section_4")
- war.gov URL: https://www.war.gov/medialink/ufo/release_1/65_hs1-834228961_62-hq-83894_section_4.pdf

**Page reference:** Pages 109–113 (FBI summary, Colonel C. D. Gasser's Air Force analysis of negatives, technical explanation of film artifact)

**Hook:** An Oak Ridge resident photographed what he reported as a flying object in July 1947. The Air Force analysis found the "sphere" and "trail" were caused by a drop of water or developer rolling across the film during processing — but the FBI and AEC still needed to document and archive the finding.

**The find:** William Presley of Oak Ridge reported photographing an unidentified object in July 1947. The photographs were forwarded to the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission, which requested analysis. Colonel C. D. Gasser, USAF AMC Engineering Field Officer, examined the negatives. His detailed technical explanation (pages 112–113) is direct: when negatives are examined by "strong incident light," the emulsion shows a raised spot at the location of the reported "sphere," consistent with a drop of warm water or developer that rolled down the film. The "trail" is continuous across two negatives, suggesting the drop rolled from one frame to the next. The fogged edge of the negative extends beyond the normal film format, indicating the "trail" was present on areas never exposed in the camera. Gasser concludes with a recommendation to declassify the matter and advise Presley of the nature of the images. The FBI summary (page 109) notes: "Colonel GASSER personally advised that while the photographs are not real, his statements made concerning the existence of flying saucers and the opinions expressed by him nevertheless stand as previously stated." This is a document about how false evidence is processed, analyzed, archived, and communicated — and how skeptical analysis does not preclude the observer's broader conviction.

**Why this not that:** This entry demonstrates scientific skepticism applied to a false claim *within an unclassified document*. It shows the forensic methodology available in 1949. It also preserves Gasser's nuance: the negatives are artifacts, but his professional confidence in UAP phenomena remains. Readers see exactly how film artifacts can mimic objects, and also how institutional response to hoaxes is methodical rather than dismissive.
